Mathematics and AI

Mathematics for AI and AI for mathematics: groups and representations, steerable kernels, grokking, category theory and lenses, sheaves on graphs, Lean 4, AI theorem proving, autoformalization and AI-driven mathematical discovery.

  • Group theory
  • Category theory
  • Sheaves
  • Lean 4
  • Theorem proving

Sessions

  1. 01

    Groups and their actions

    Build C4, D4 and Sn as concrete objects, check the group axioms by computer, and test which models respect which symmetries.

    Read notebook Colab Kaggle .ipynb Runs fully on CPU
  2. 02

    Representations and irreducibles

    Decompose a representation of a small group into irreducible pieces with characters and projectors, then split a product of two vectors under rotations.

    Read notebook Colab Kaggle .ipynb 1 NOT RUN cell
  3. 03

    Steerable kernels

    Solve the kernel constraint numerically, look at the basis of allowed kernels, and build an equivariant convolution from it.

    Read notebook Colab Kaggle .ipynb Runs fully on CPU
  4. 04

    Mechanistic interpretability with mathematics: grokking

    Train a small network on addition modulo a prime, watch it generalize long after it has memorized, and find the Fourier features it learned.

    Read notebook Colab Kaggle .ipynb Runs fully on CPU
  5. 05

    Category theory for deep learning I

    Build a small category whose objects are tensor shapes and whose arrows are layers, and check the laws with tests.

    Read notebook Colab Kaggle .ipynb Runs fully on CPU
  6. 06

    Category theory for deep learning II: lenses and backprop

    Write automatic differentiation as composition of lenses, check it against finite differences, and train a small model with it.

    Read notebook Colab Kaggle .ipynb Runs fully on CPU
  7. 07

    Architectures from structure

    Derive weight sharing by solving 'commute with the symmetry' as a linear system, and check the structural law behind CNNs, RNNs and GNNs.

    Read notebook Colab Kaggle .ipynb Runs fully on CPU
  8. 08

    Sheaves on graphs

    Build a sheaf Laplacian, see why it does not oversmooth the way the graph Laplacian does, and compare sheaf diffusion with a GCN on a heterophilic graph.

    Read notebook Colab Kaggle .ipynb Runs fully on CPU
  9. 09

    Proof assistants: first proofs in Lean 4

    State and prove two small theorems about groups and one about categories in Lean 4, and understand what a proof assistant checks that a test cannot.

    Read notebook Colab Kaggle .ipynb 2 NOT RUN cells
  10. 10

    AI for theorem proving

    Understand proof search and the pass@k metric with a small runnable model, then plan an evaluation of an open prover on miniF2F.

    Read notebook Colab Kaggle .ipynb 1 NOT RUN cell
  11. 11

    Autoformalization

    Practise catching formal statements that do not say what the informal statement means, then autoformalize ten textbook statements into Lean.

    Read notebook Colab Kaggle .ipynb 1 NOT RUN cell
  12. 12

    AI for mathematical discovery

    Build a small evolve-and-evaluate loop that searches for programs producing large cap sets, with a trusted evaluator and a pluggable mutation step.

    Read notebook Colab Kaggle .ipynb 1 NOT RUN cell

Before you start

One notebook per session. Open them in Kaggle Notebooks or Google Colab.

  • Every Python cell without a NOT RUN label was run once on a CPU on 17 November 2026 (Python 3, NumPy 2.5, scikit-learn 1.9, PyTorch 2.15).
  • Sessions 1 to 8 and 12 are fully runnable: groups and actions, characters and projectors, steerable kernel bases, grokking with Fourier analysis, a category of layers, lenses as backprop, weight sharing derived from symmetry, sheaf diffusion, and an evolve-and-evaluate search for cap sets.
  • Lean code (sessions 9 and 11) was NOT compiled. It follows Lean 4 and Mathlib conventions as of 2026, but Mathlib renames lemmas often. Compile every snippet before teaching it, and treat any error as part of the lab.
  • Sessions 10 and 11 give runnable models of the ideas (proof search, pass@k, counterexample search for wrong formalizations). The parts that need a prover model, LeanDojo or a language model are labelled NOT RUN.
  • Session 4 uses p = 47 and a small network so that grokking appears in about a minute on a CPU. The paper it follows uses p = 113 and a one-layer transformer.
  • Session 12 searches in dimension 5 with random edits in place of a language model. In the run recorded here, random search with the same budget did as well as the evolutionary loop; the notebook says so.
