1. By hand first: how far apart are floating-point numbers?¶

A floating-point number is stored as a sign, an exponent and a fraction with $t$ bits: $x = \pm(1.f_1f_2\ldots f_t)_2 \times 2^e$. Between $2^e$ and $2^{e+1}$ the numbers are evenly spaced, $2^{e-t}$ apart. Machine epsilon $\varepsilon = 2^{-t}$ is the gap between 1 and the next number; rounding to the nearest number makes a relative error of at most $\varepsilon/2$. Ties round to the number whose last bit is 0 ("round half to even").

float16 has $t = 10$ fraction bits and bfloat16 has $t = 7$. On paper: what is the spacing of float16 numbers between 2048 and 4096, and so what is $2048 + 1$ in float16? What is $256 + 1$ in bfloat16? Then run the cell, which also prints the main facts about each format.

2. Overflow, underflow and mixed precision¶

float16 overflows above 65,504, and anything below about $6 \times 10^{-8}$ underflows to 0. Mixed-precision training (Micikevicius et al. 2018) computes in float16 but guards against both failures:

  • Loss scaling: multiply the loss by a scale $S$ before the backward pass, so small gradients become representable in float16, then divide the gradients by $S$ in float32.
  • float32 master weights: an update much smaller than the weight is rounded away in float16, because $1 + u$ rounds to 1 when $u \le 2^{-11} \approx 4.9 \times 10^{-4}$.

bfloat16 keeps float32's 8 exponent bits, so it has float32's range and usually needs no loss scaling, but it keeps only 7 fraction bits (Kalamkar et al. 2019).

3. Stable softmax and log-sum-exp¶

$e^x$ overflows float64 for $x > 709.78$ and float32 for $x > 88.72$. Softmax does not change when the same number is subtracted from every input, $\text{softmax}(x) = \text{softmax}(x - m)$, so subtract $m = \max_i x_i$ first. In the same way, $\log\sum_i e^{x_i} = m + \log\sum_i e^{x_i - m}$ (the log-sum-exp trick). The cross-entropy of logits $z$ with label $y$ is then $\text{logsumexp}(z) - z_y$, which never forms the probabilities at all; this is why PyTorch's cross_entropy takes logits.

def softmax_naive(x):  e = np.exp(x); return e / e.sum()
def softmax_stable(x): e = np.exp(x - x.max()); return e / e.sum()
def lse_naive(x):  return np.log(np.exp(x).sum())
def lse_stable(x): m = x.max(); return m + np.log(np.exp(x - m).sum())

with np.errstate(over="ignore", invalid="ignore", divide="ignore"):
    for x in [np.array([1.0, 2.0]), np.array([1000.0, 1001.0]), np.array([-1000.0, -1001.0])]:
        print(f"x = {x}: softmax naive {softmax_naive(x)}, stable {np.round(softmax_stable(x), 4)}; "

4. Stable sigmoid, softplus and binary cross-entropy¶

The softplus function $\log(1 + e^x)$ appears in every logistic loss. Two things go wrong with the naive formula: $e^x$ overflows for large $x$, and for very negative $x$ the sum $1 + e^x$ rounds to exactly 1, so the answer becomes 0 instead of about $e^x$. The stable form is $\max(x, 0) + \text{log1p}(e^{-|x|})$, where log1p(u) computes $\log(1 + u)$ accurately for tiny $u$. NumPy's np.logaddexp(0, x) does this for you.

Binary cross-entropy from a logit $z$ with label $y \in \{0, 1\}$ simplifies to $\text{softplus}(z) - yz$, again without forming $\sigma(z)$; PyTorch's BCEWithLogitsLoss works from logits for the same reason.

def softplus_naive(x):  return np.log(1 + np.exp(x))
def softplus_stable(x): return np.maximum(x, 0) + np.log1p(np.exp(-np.abs(x)))

print("      x |  naive log(1 + e^x) | stable               | np.logaddexp(0, x)")
with np.errstate(over="ignore"):

        print(f"{x:7.0f} | {softplus_naive(x):19.6g} | {softplus_stable(x):20.6g} | {np.logaddexp(0, x):.6g}")
print(f"log(1 + 1e-20) = {np.log(1 + 1e-20)}, log1p(1e-20) = {np.log1p(1e-20)}")

5. Catastrophic cancellation: the variance formula¶

Subtracting two nearly equal numbers keeps their absolute rounding error but cancels their leading digits, so the relative error of the difference can be huge. The one-pass formula $\text{Var}(x) = E[x^2] - (E[x])^2$ does exactly that when the mean is large compared with the spread. The two-pass formula $E[(x - \bar x)^2]$ subtracts the mean first and is safe; np.var uses it.

By hand: for the data 10001, 10002, 10003 in float32, $x^2 \approx 10^8$, where float32 numbers are 8 apart. Can $E[x^2] - (E[x])^2$ come out as 0.667? A second example: $(1 - \cos x)/x^2 \to 1/2$ as $x \to 0$, but for $x = 10^{-8}$ the computed $\cos x$ is exactly 1. The identity $1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2(x/2)$ removes the subtraction.

for dtype, base in [(np.float32, 1e4), (np.float64, 1e8)]:
    x = (base + np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])).astype(dtype)
    one_pass = np.mean(x * x) - np.mean(x) ** 2
    two_pass = np.mean((x - np.mean(x)) ** 2)
    print(f"{np.dtype(dtype).name}, data {base:.0e} + (1, 2, 3): one-pass {float(one_pass):.4f}, two-pass {float(two_pass):.4f}, "
          f"np.var {float(np.var(x)):.4f} (true 0.6667)")
xc = 1e-8
print(f"(1 - cos x)/x^2 at x = 1e-8: direct {(1 - np.cos(xc)) / xc**2}, with 2 sin^2(x/2) {2 * np.sin(xc / 2) ** 2 / xc**2} (true 0.5)")

6. Conditioning against stability: normal equations or QR?¶

Conditioning belongs to the problem: the condition number $\kappa(A) = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ bounds how much relative errors in the data can grow in the answer. As a rule of thumb, you lose about $\log_{10}\kappa$ of float64's 16 digits. Stability belongs to the algorithm: a backward-stable algorithm gives the exact answer to a slightly perturbed problem (Trefethen & Bau 1997).

For least squares, the normal equations $A^\top A x = A^\top y$ work with $\kappa(A^\top A) = \kappa(A)^2$, so they lose about twice as many digits as needed. Householder QR (np.linalg.qr) and the SVD (np.linalg.lstsq) work with $A$ itself. We fit polynomials of growing degree to exact data on 100 points in [0, 1] with the Vandermonde matrix $A_{ij} = x_i^{\,j}$ and true coefficients all equal to 1, and measure the relative error of the coefficients.

xg = np.linspace(0, 1, 100)
err = lambda c, c0: np.linalg.norm(c - c0) / np.linalg.norm(c0)
rows = []
print("degree |  cond(A)  | relative error: normal equations |    QR    | lstsq (SVD)")
for deg in [3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15]:
    A = np.vander(xg, deg + 1, increasing=True)
    c0 = np.ones(deg + 1); y = A @ c0
    kappa = np.linalg.cond(A)
    c_ne = np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ y)              # normal equations: no error message, whatever happens
    Q, R = np.linalg.qr(A); c_qr = linalg.solve_triangular(R, Q.T @ y)
    c_ls = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y, rcond=None)[0]
    rows.append((deg, kappa, err(c_ne, c0), err(c_qr, c0), err(c_ls, c0)))
    print(f"{deg:6d} | {kappa:9.2e} | {rows[-1][2]:32.2e} | {rows[-1][3]:8.2e} | {rows[-1][4]:.2e}")

7. Conjugate gradient for large sparse systems¶

A dense solver needs all $n^2$ entries and about $n^3/3$ operations. Many large systems are sparse instead, such as a graph Laplacian (session 12) with a few nonzeros per row. The conjugate gradient (CG) method solves $Ax = b$ for symmetric positive definite $A$ using only products $Av$. In exact arithmetic it finishes in at most $n$ steps, and the error after $k$ steps is at most $2\left(\frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}\right)^k$ times the starting error, measured in the norm $\|e\|_A = \sqrt{e^\top A e}$ (Trefethen & Bau 1997). The $\sqrt\kappa$ is the same as for momentum in session 8.

We take the Laplacian $L$ of a 50 × 50 grid graph ($n = 2{,}500$ unknowns) and solve $(L + sI)x = b$ for two shifts: $s = 0.1$ ($\kappa \approx 81$) and $s = 0.001$ ($\kappa \approx 8{,}000$). At $n = 10^6$ a dense matrix would need 8 TB, while CG needs a few vectors.

m = 50
path = sparse.diags([-np.ones(m - 1), np.r_[1.0, 2.0 * np.ones(m - 2), 1.0], -np.ones(m - 1)], [-1, 0, 1])   # path-graph Laplacian
Lg = (sparse.kron(path, sparse.identity(m)) + sparse.kron(sparse.identity(m), path)).tocsr()               # 50 x 50 grid
b = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=m * m)

def cg(A, b, tol=1e-10, max_iter=10000):
    x = np.zeros_like(b); r = b.copy(); p = r.copy(); rs = r @ r; hist = [1.0]
    for k in range(max_iter):
        Ap = A @ p; alpha = rs / (p @ Ap)
        x += alpha * p; r -= alpha * Ap
        rs_new = r @ r; hist.append(np.sqrt(rs_new) / np.linalg.norm(b))
        if hist[-1] < tol: break
        p = r + (rs_new / rs) * p; rs = rs_new
    return x, np.array(hist)

lam_max = 4 * (1 + np.cos(np.pi / m))                    # largest eigenvalue of the grid Laplacian
grid_eigs = np.sort((4 - 2 * np.cos(np.pi * np.arange(m) / m)[:, None] - 2 * np.cos(np.pi * np.arange(m) / m)[None, :]).ravel())
print(f"grid Laplacian: largest eigenvalue {lam_max:.4f}; four smallest {np.round(grid_eigs[:4], 5)} (0 belongs to the constant vector)")
hists = {}
for s in [0.1, 0.001]:
    A = (Lg + s * sparse.identity(m * m)).tocsr()
    t0 = time.perf_counter(); x_cg, hist = cg(A, b); t_cg = time.perf_counter() - t0
    Ad = A.toarray()
    t0 = time.perf_counter(); x_dir = np.linalg.solve(Ad, b); t_dir = time.perf_counter() - t0
    kappa = (lam_max + s) / s; hists[s] = (kappa, hist)
    print(f"shift {s}: kappa = {kappa:.0f}, sqrt(kappa) = {np.sqrt(kappa):.1f}; CG reached residual 1e-10 in {len(hist) - 1} iterations "
          f"({1000 * t_cg:.0f} ms); dense solve {1000 * t_dir:.0f} ms; largest difference {np.abs(x_cg - x_dir).max():.1e}")
print(f"memory: dense matrix {Ad.nbytes / 1e6:.0f} MB, sparse matrix {(A.data.nbytes + A.indices.nbytes + A.indptr.nbytes) / 1e3:.0f} kB ({A.nnz} nonzeros)")

plt.figure(figsize=(6.5, 3.3))
for s, (kappa, hist) in hists.items():
    plt.semilogy(hist, label=f"shift {s}, kappa = {kappa:.0f}")
plt.xlabel("CG iteration"); plt.ylabel("relative residual ||b - Ax|| / ||b||"); plt.title("conjugate gradient on the grid Laplacian, n = 2,500", fontsize=10)
plt.legend(fontsize=8); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
print(f"runtime so far {time.time() - t_start:.0f} s")

8. Mixed-precision training on a GPU (NOT RUN here)¶

PyTorch's automatic mixed precision runs matrix products in float16 or bfloat16 inside torch.autocast, keeps float32 master weights, and GradScaler adjusts the loss scale as training goes: lower after an overflow, higher after a run of good steps. It needs a CUDA GPU.

# NOT RUN: needs a CUDA GPU (float16 autocast and loss scaling run on the GPU)
import torch.nn as nn
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
Xd, yd = load_digits(return_X_y=True)
X_gpu = torch.tensor(Xd / 16.0, dtype=torch.float32, device="cuda"); y_gpu = torch.tensor(yd, device="cuda")
model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, 256), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(256, 10)).to("cuda")     # weights stay float32
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda")
for step in range(500):
    idx = torch.randint(0, len(X_gpu), (128,), device="cuda")
    with torch.autocast(device_type="cuda", dtype=torch.float16):        # for bfloat16, use dtype=torch.bfloat16 and no scaler
        loss = nn.functional.cross_entropy(model(X_gpu[idx]), y_gpu[idx])
    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    scaler.scale(loss).backward()          # gradients of (scale x loss)
    scaler.step(opt)                       # unscales the gradients; skips the step if any is inf or nan
    scaler.update()                        # adjusts the scale for the next step
print(f"final loss {loss.item():.4f}, final loss scale {scaler.get_scale():.0f}")

What to notice¶

(Written from the run above; your numbers will match, except the timings.)

  1. The spacing of the numbers decides what can be stored. In float16, 2048 + 1 = 2048; in bfloat16, 256 + 1 = 256; in float32, $2^{24} + 1 = 2^{24}$. At those sizes the gap between neighbouring numbers is 2, and the tie rounds to even. Machine epsilon was 2.2204e-16, 1.1921e-07, 9.7656e-04 and 7.8125e-03 for float64, float32, float16 and bfloat16, and the largest numbers 1.7977e+308, 3.4028e+38, 65,504 and 3.3895e+38.
  2. float16 fails at both ends, and mixed precision patches both. 300 × 300 overflowed to inf in float16 but gave 90112 in bfloat16 (range kept, precision lost). A gradient of 1e-8 became 0 in float16; with a loss scale of 1,024 it came back as 1.0012e-08, and with 65,536 as 9.9972e-09. Ten thousand updates of 1e-4 left a float16 weight at exactly 1.0, while a float32 weight reached 2.000166.
  3. Naive formulas fail, sometimes with no warning. Softmax of (1000, 1001) gave nan, and (0.2689, 0.7311) after subtracting the maximum; log-sum-exp gave inf against 1001.3133. In float32 the naive softmax already failed at logits (100, 101). Naive softplus returned 0 at $x = -40$ (true value 4.25e-18) and inf at $x = 1000$, and a logit of 40 with label 0 gave an infinite binary cross-entropy instead of 40.
  4. Cancellation made a variance negative. The one-pass formula gave −8.0000 for the float32 data $10^4 + (1, 2, 3)$ and 0.0000 for the float64 data $10^8 + (1, 2, 3)$; the two-pass formula and np.var gave 0.6667 both times. $(1 - \cos x)/x^2$ at $x = 10^{-8}$ gave 0.0 instead of 0.5.
  5. The normal equations square the condition number. QR's error stayed below $\kappa\varepsilon$ at every degree (5.86e-06 at $\kappa$ = 1.31e11), while the normal equations followed $\kappa^2\varepsilon$ until the answer was meaningless: relative errors of 2.47e-01 at degree 11, 1.11e+01 at degree 13 and 3.72e+00 at degree 15, with no warning or error message.
  6. CG beat the dense solver easily: 100 and 246 iterations of a few milliseconds in total, much faster than the dense solve (timings vary from run to run), 158 kB of matrix instead of 50 MB, and the same answer to within 6.3e-10. Against expectation, raising $\kappa$ from 81 to 7,993 ($\sqrt\kappa$ from 9.0 to 89.4) raised the iteration count only 2.5 times, not about 10 times. The $\sqrt\kappa$ formula is only an upper bound that looks at the two extreme eigenvalues; CG adapts to all of them. Here only one eigenvalue equals the small shift (the constant vector's); the next smallest Laplacian eigenvalues are 0.00395, so after the shift they are about five times larger. The residual for the small shift also stalled for about 100 iterations: CG minimizes the error measured in the $A$-norm, and the residual need not fall at every step.
  7. These systems are small. At $n = 10^6$ unknowns a dense matrix would need 8 TB, while CG still needs only a few vectors.
