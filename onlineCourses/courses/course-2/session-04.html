1. By hand first: partial derivatives and the gradient¶

A partial derivative $\partial f / \partial x_i$ is the slope of $f$ when only $x_i$ moves. The gradient $\nabla f$ collects all of them in a vector. A central difference checks any of them numerically: $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} \approx \frac{f(x + h e_i) - f(x - h e_i)}{2h}.$$

By hand first:

  1. $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + 3x_2^2$. What is $\nabla f(1, 1)$?
  2. Logistic regression on one example: $z = w \cdot x$, $p = \sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$ and loss $L = -[y \log p + (1 - y)\log(1 - p)]$. Its gradient is $\nabla_w L = (p - y)\,x$ (derived in section 5). For $x = (1, 2)$, $y = 1$ and $w = (0, 0)$, what is $\nabla_w L$?
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer, make_blobs

np.random.seed(0)
np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)

def numerical_gradient(f, w, h=1e-5):
    # central differences, one coordinate at a time
    w = np.asarray(w, dtype=float)
    g = np.zeros_like(w)
    for i in range(w.size):
        e = np.zeros_like(w); e.flat[i] = h
        g.flat[i] = (f(w + e) - f(w - e)) / (2 * h)
    return g

def rel_error(a, b):
    return np.linalg.norm(a - b) / max(np.linalg.norm(a) + np.linalg.norm(b), 1e-300)

sigmoid = lambda z: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

f = lambda v: v[0] ** 2 + 3 * v[1] ** 2
print("gradient of x1^2 + 3 x2^2 at (1, 1): by hand (2, 6); numerically", numerical_gradient(f, [1.0, 1.0]))

x1, y1 = np.array([1.0, 2.0]), 1.0
loss_one = lambda w: -(y1 * np.log(sigmoid(w @ x1)) + (1 - y1) * np.log(1 - sigmoid(w @ x1)))
print("logistic loss gradient at w = 0: formula", (sigmoid(0.0) - y1) * x1, "; numerically", numerical_gradient(loss_one, [0.0, 0.0]))

2. The gradient points uphill, at right angles to the contours¶

A contour is a curve on which $f$ is constant. Moving along it does not change $f$, so the gradient is perpendicular to it and points to where $f$ increases fastest. Gradient descent steps the other way, $w \leftarrow w - \eta \nabla f(w)$.

Left: the loss of a two-weight logistic regression on 200 overlapping points, with arrows for $-\nabla L$ and a gradient-descent path. Right: $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + 10x_2^2$ at the point (10, 1), where the steepest-descent direction and the direction to the minimum are far apart (see the Watch out in the course document).

Xb, yb = make_blobs(n_samples=200, centers=[[-1, -1], [1, 1]], cluster_std=1.2, random_state=0)
yb = yb.astype(float)
loss2 = lambda w: np.mean(np.logaddexp(0, Xb @ w) - yb * (Xb @ w))      # log(1 + e^z) - y z, written stably
grad2 = lambda w: Xb.T @ (sigmoid(Xb @ w) - yb) / len(yb)

w = np.array([-2.0, 2.5]); path = [w.copy()]
for _ in range(30):
    w = w - 1.0 * grad2(w)
    path.append(w.copy())

print(f"gradient descent: loss {loss2(path[0]):.4f} -> {loss2(path[-1]):.4f}; weights after 30 steps {path[-1]}")

3. Three matrix-calculus rules you actually need¶

With the gradient as a column vector:

  • $\nabla_x (a^\top x) = a$;
  • $\nabla_x (x^\top A x) = (A + A^\top)\,x$, which is $2Ax$ when $A$ is symmetric;
  • $\nabla_x \|Ax - b\|^2 = 2A^\top (Ax - b)$, the gradient behind the normal equations of session 2.

By hand first: for $A$ with rows (1, 2) and (0, 3), write $x^\top A x$ as a polynomial in $x_1, x_2$, differentiate it, and evaluate at $x = (1, 1)$. Compare with $(A + A^\top)x$.

A_h = np.array([[1.0, 2.0], [0.0, 3.0]])
print("grad of x^T A x at (1, 1): (A + A^T) x =", (A_h + A_h.T) @ np.ones(2), "; numerically", numerical_gradient(lambda v: v @ A_h @ v, np.ones(2)))

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
a, x0 = rng.standard_normal(4), rng.standard_normal(4)
A4, M, bm = rng.standard_normal((4, 4)), rng.standard_normal((6, 4)), rng.standard_normal(6)
checks = [("a^T x", lambda v: a @ v, a),
          ("x^T A x", lambda v: v @ A4 @ v, (A4 + A4.T) @ x0),
          ("||M x - b||^2", lambda v: np.sum((M @ v - bm) ** 2), 2 * M.T @ (M @ x0 - bm))]
for name, fn, formula in checks:
    print(f"{name:14s} relative error between formula and central differences: {rel_error(formula, numerical_gradient(fn, x0)):.1e}")

4. Jacobians and the chain rule¶

For a function $g: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, the Jacobian $J_g$ is the $m \times n$ matrix of partial derivatives $\partial g_i / \partial x_j$. The chain rule multiplies Jacobians: $J_{f \circ g}(x) = J_f(g(x))\, J_g(x)$.

By hand first: the softmax $p_i = e^{z_i} / \sum_j e^{z_j}$ has Jacobian $\partial p_i / \partial z_j = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)$, that is $J = \operatorname{diag}(p) - p p^\top$. For three classes at $z = (0, 0, 0)$, $p = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$: write down $J$. Then, with cross-entropy loss $-\log p_y$ for true class $y = 0$, use $\nabla_z = J^\top \nabla_p$ with $\nabla_p = (-1/p_0, 0, 0)$ to get the gradient with respect to $z$. It should equal $p - \text{onehot}(y)$.

def softmax(z):
    e = np.exp(z - z.max())
    return e / e.sum()

def numerical_jacobian(g, x, h=1e-5):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    return np.column_stack([(g(x + h * e) - g(x - h * e)) / (2 * h) for e in np.eye(x.size)])

z0 = np.zeros(3)
p0 = softmax(z0)
J = np.diag(p0) - np.outer(p0, p0)
print("softmax Jacobian at z = 0 (2/9 = 0.2222, -1/9 = -0.1111):\n", J)
print("rows sum to", J.sum(axis=1), "; agrees with finite differences:", np.allclose(J, numerical_jacobian(softmax, z0), atol=1e-9))
grad_p = np.array([-1 / p0[0], 0.0, 0.0])
print("chain rule J^T grad_p =", J.T @ grad_p, "; p - onehot =", p0 - np.eye(3)[0],
      "; numerically", numerical_gradient(lambda z: -np.log(softmax(z)[0]), z0))

W = rng.standard_normal((3, 4)); xv = rng.standard_normal(4)
J_chain = np.diag(1 - np.tanh(W @ xv) ** 2) @ W           # J_tanh(Wx) times J_(Wx) = W
print(f"tanh(Wx): relative error between the chain-rule Jacobian and finite differences {rel_error(J_chain, numerical_jacobian(lambda v: np.tanh(W @ v), xv)):.1e}")

5. The gradient of the logistic-regression loss, derived and checked¶

For data $X$ ($n \times d$, with a column of ones for the bias), labels $y \in \{0, 1\}$ and $z = Xw$: $$L(w) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big[\log(1 + e^{z_i}) - y_i z_i\big],$$ which is the same as the cross-entropy $-[y \log \sigma(z) + (1 - y)\log(1 - \sigma(z))]$ averaged over examples. Since $\frac{d}{dz}\log(1 + e^z) = \sigma(z)$, we get $\partial L / \partial z_i = (\sigma(z_i) - y_i)/n$, and the chain rule through $z = Xw$ gives $$\nabla_w L = \frac{1}{n} X^\top (\sigma(Xw) - y).$$ We check it on the breast-cancer data (569 patients, 30 standardized features), then plant three common bugs.

bc = load_breast_cancer()
Xc = (bc.data - bc.data.mean(0)) / bc.data.std(0)
Xc = np.column_stack([Xc, np.ones(len(Xc))])             # bias column
yc = bc.target.astype(float)
loss = lambda w: np.mean(np.logaddexp(0, Xc @ w) - yc * (Xc @ w))
grad = lambda w: Xc.T @ (sigmoid(Xc @ w) - yc) / len(yc)

w0 = rng.normal(0, 0.1, Xc.shape[1])
g_num = numerical_gradient(loss, w0)
print(f"relative error, correct gradient: {rel_error(grad(w0), g_num):.1e}")
bugs = {"sign flipped (y - p)": lambda w: Xc.T @ (yc - sigmoid(Xc @ w)) / len(yc),
        "forgot to divide by n": lambda w: Xc.T @ (sigmoid(Xc @ w) - yc),
        "extra factor sigma'(z)": lambda w: Xc.T @ ((sigmoid(Xc @ w) - yc) * sigmoid(Xc @ w) * (1 - sigmoid(Xc @ w))) / len(yc)}
for name, g_bug in bugs.items():
    print(f"relative error, bug '{name}': {rel_error(g_bug(w0), g_num):.3f}")

relative error, correct gradient: 9.1e-12
relative error, bug 'sign flipped (y - p)': 1.000
relative error, bug 'forgot to divide by n': 0.996
relative error, bug 'extra factor sigma'(z)': 0.612

Why a small step against the gradient lowers the loss. Taylor's theorem says $L(w + \delta) = L(w) + \nabla L(w)^\top \delta + O(\|\delta\|^2)$. With $\delta = -\eta \nabla L$ the first-order change is $-\eta\|\nabla L\|^2 < 0$, and for small enough $\eta$ the $O(\eta^2)$ term cannot cancel it. We compare the actual decrease with the first-order prediction, then run gradient descent.

g0 = grad(w0)
for eta in [1.0, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001]:
    actual = loss(w0) - loss(w0 - eta * g0)
    predicted = eta * g0 @ g0
    print(f"step size {eta:<6}: actual decrease {actual:.6f}, first-order prediction {predicted:.6f}, ratio {actual / predicted:.4f}")

w = np.zeros(Xc.shape[1])
for _ in range(500):
    w -= 0.5 * grad(w)
print(f"after 500 gradient-descent steps: training loss {loss(w):.4f}, training accuracy {np.mean((Xc @ w > 0) == (yc == 1)):.3f}, gradient norm {np.linalg.norm(grad(w)):.1e}")

6. Choosing the step $h$: truncation against rounding¶

A finite difference has two errors. Truncation: Taylor's theorem gives an error of about $\frac{h}{2}|f''|$ for the forward difference $(f(x + h) - f(x))/h$ and about $\frac{h^2}{6}|f^{(3)}|$ for the central difference. Rounding: each value of $f$ carries a relative error of about $\varepsilon \approx 10^{-16}$ in float64, and dividing their difference by $h$ amplifies it to about $\varepsilon|f|/h$. Too large an $h$ gives truncation error, too small an $h$ gives rounding error. We test $f = \sin$ at $x = 1$, where $f'(1) = \cos 1$.

hs = 10.0 ** -np.arange(1, 16)
fwd = np.abs((np.sin(1 + hs) - np.sin(1)) / hs - np.cos(1))
cen = np.abs((np.sin(1 + hs) - np.sin(1 - hs)) / (2 * hs) - np.cos(1))
one32 = np.float32(1.0)
cen32 = np.array([abs(float((np.sin(one32 + np.float32(h)) - np.sin(one32 - np.float32(h))) / np.float32(2 * h)) - np.cos(1.0)) for h in hs])
for name, err in [("forward, float64", fwd), ("central, float64", cen), ("central, float32", cen32)]:
    print(f"{name}: smallest error {err.min():.1e} at h = {hs[err.argmin()]:.0e}")

eps = np.finfo(float).eps
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 3.5))
ax.loglog(hs, fwd, "o-", ms=3, label="forward difference, float64")
ax.loglog(hs, cen, "s-", ms=3, label="central difference, float64")
ax.loglog(hs, cen32, "^-", ms=3, label="central difference, float32")
ax.loglog(hs, hs / 2 * np.sin(1), "k:", lw=0.8, label="h |f''| / 2 (truncation, forward)")
ax.loglog(hs, hs ** 2 / 6 * np.cos(1), "k--", lw=0.8, label="h^2 |f(3)| / 6 (truncation, central)")
ax.loglog(hs, eps * np.sin(1) / hs, "k-.", lw=0.8, label="eps |f| / h (rounding)")
ax.set_ylim(1e-13, 1); ax.invert_xaxis(); ax.legend(fontsize=7)
ax.set(title="Error of the finite-difference derivative of sin at x = 1", xlabel="step h (smaller to the right)", ylabel="absolute error")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

8. The same check with JAX (NOT RUN here)¶

JAX computes gradients and Hessians of NumPy-style code automatically; session 5 explains how.

# NOT RUN: needs JAX (pip install jax), which is not installed here.
import jax
import jax.numpy as jnp
jax.config.update("jax_enable_x64", True)                  # float64, so the comparison is tight

def loss_jax(w, X, y):
    z = X @ w
    return jnp.mean(jnp.logaddexp(0.0, z) - y * z)

g_jax = jax.grad(loss_jax)(jnp.array(w0), jnp.array(Xc), jnp.array(yc))
H_jax = jax.hessian(loss_jax)(jnp.array(w0), jnp.array(Xc), jnp.array(yc))
print("largest difference from our formula:", float(jnp.max(jnp.abs(g_jax - grad(w0)))))
print("Hessian shape:", H_jax.shape, "; smallest eigenvalue (convexity, session 8):", float(jnp.linalg.eigvalsh(H_jax).min()))

What to notice¶

  • The hand calculations matched (sections 1, 3, 4): $\nabla(x_1^2 + 3x_2^2) = (2, 6)$ at (1, 1); the logistic gradient at $w = 0$ is (−0.5, −1); $(A + A^\top)x = (4, 8)$; the softmax Jacobian at $z = 0$ has 0.2222 (2/9) on the diagonal and −0.1111 (−1/9) elsewhere, with rows summing to 0; and the chain rule gave (−0.6667, 0.3333, 0.3333), exactly $p - \text{onehot}(y)$.
  • The gradient is perpendicular to the contours (section 2). The descent path crossed every contour at a right angle and lowered the loss from 1.5478 to 0.3266 in 30 steps. On $x_1^2 + 10x_2^2$ at (10, 1), the steepest-descent direction was 39.29° away from the direction to the minimum.
  • Formulas against central differences. The three matrix-calculus rules and the tanh chain rule agreed to relative errors between 3.8e-12 and 1.1e-11, and the logistic-regression gradient to 9.1e-12. The planted bugs gave 1.000 (sign), 0.996 (no division by $n$) and 0.612 (an extra $\sigma'$): a correct gradient and a wrong one are about ten orders of magnitude apart.
  • Taylor's prediction is a small-step statement (section 5). The actual decrease divided by the first-order prediction was 0.2493, 0.8514, 0.9847 and 0.9985 for step sizes 1, 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001. At step size 1 the loss still fell, but by a quarter of the prediction. Five hundred descent steps reached a training loss of 0.0531 and training accuracy of 0.988.
  • The step size h has a best value (section 6). Forward differences were best at $h = 10^{-8}$ (error 3.0e-09) and central differences at $h = 10^{-5}$ (error 1.1e-11), as the balance of truncation and rounding predicts. In float32 the best central error was only 1.0e-05 (at $h = 10^{-2}$), and below $h \approx 10^{-7}$ the float32 difference was exactly 0, an error of 0.54 = cos 1. Do gradient checks in float64.
  • These checks cost two loss evaluations per parameter, so on a large network check a random sample of coordinates, or a few directions, rather than every weight.
