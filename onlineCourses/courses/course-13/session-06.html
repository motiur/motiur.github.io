1. Thirty years of synthetic daily weather at one station¶

Weather is the state of the atmosphere on a given day; climate is the statistics of weather over many years (the typical value for a date, and how much it varies). We build 30 years (1995–2024) of daily data for one made-up lowland station with a monsoon climate:

  • temperature = a seasonal cycle (cool winter, hot pre-monsoon, long warm monsoon) + an anomaly that follows an autoregressive process: today's anomaly depends on the last two days' anomalies plus noise, so warm spells last a few days;
  • rainfall: whether it rains follows a two-state Markov chain (the chance of rain tomorrow depends on whether it rained today), with much higher chances in the monsoon; amounts on wet days follow a gamma distribution.

Real station records, from a national weather service such as the Bangladesh Meteorological Department, or a reanalysis such as ERA5 (section 6), would replace these numbers. We train on 1995–2014 and test on 2015–2024.

import time
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, LogisticRegression
from sklearn.calibration import calibration_curve

t_start = time.time()
rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
dates = pd.date_range("1995-01-01", "2024-12-31", freq="D")
doy, n = dates.dayofyear.to_numpy(), len(dates)
w = 2 * np.pi * (doy - 1) / 365.25
clim_true = 26.0 - 5.0 * np.cos(w - 0.25) - 1.2 * np.cos(2 * (w - 0.3))     # deg C, the true seasonal cycle
sig = 0.55 * (1.3 + 0.5 * np.cos(w))                                        # anomalies vary more in winter
a = np.zeros(n)
eps = rng.normal(0, 1, n) * sig
for t in range(2, n):
    a[t] = 1.2 * a[t - 1] - 0.3 * a[t - 2] + eps[t]                          # AR(2) anomaly
temp = clim_true + a

monsoon = np.exp(-((doy - 200) / 55.0) ** 2)
p01, p11 = 0.05 + 0.45 * monsoon, 0.30 + 0.50 * monsoon                     # P(wet | dry yesterday), P(wet | wet yesterday)
wet = np.zeros(n, bool)
u = rng.random(n)
for t in range(1, n):
    wet[t] = u[t] < (p11[t] if wet[t - 1] else p01[t])
mean_amt = 5 + 13 * monsoon
rain = np.where(wet, rng.gamma(0.75, mean_amt / 0.75), 0.0)                 # mm on wet days

train = dates.year <= 2014
test = dates.year >= 2015
print(f"{n} days; training days {train.sum()}, test days {test.sum()}")
print(f"temperature: mean {temp.mean():.1f} C, coolest day-of-year mean {pd.Series(clim_true).groupby(doy).mean().min():.1f} C, "
      f"warmest {pd.Series(clim_true).groupby(doy).mean().max():.1f} C; anomaly sd {a.std():.2f} C")
print(f"wet days: {wet.mean():.2f} of all days; July {wet[dates.month == 7].mean():.2f}, January {wet[dates.month == 1].mean():.2f}; "
      f"mean annual rainfall {rain.sum() / 30:.0f} mm")

yr = dates.year == 2020
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.2))
ax[0].plot(dates[yr], temp[yr], lw=0.8, label="daily temperature"); ax[0].plot(dates[yr], clim_true[yr], "k--", lw=1, label="seasonal cycle")
ax[0].set_ylabel("deg C"); ax[0].set_title("one synthetic year (2020): temperature", fontsize=10); ax[0].legend(fontsize=8)
ax[1].bar(dates[yr], rain[yr], width=1.0, color="C0"); ax[1].set_ylabel("mm per day"); ax[1].set_title("rainfall", fontsize=10)
for a_ in ax:
    a_.tick_params(axis="x", labelsize=7)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

2. Three temperature forecasts for 1 to 10 days ahead¶

  • Persistence: tomorrow (and every later day) will be like today.
  • Climatology: the long-term average for that date. We estimate the seasonal cycle from the training years with a harmonic regression (sines and cosines of the day of year).
  • Autoregressive (AR) model: forecast the anomaly $L$ days ahead from today's and the previous two days' anomalies with a linear regression fitted on the training years (one regression per lead time $L$), then add the climatology back.

We score with the root mean squared error (RMSE), the skill score $\mathrm{SS} = 1 - \mathrm{MSE}/\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{reference}}$ (1 is perfect, 0 is no better than the reference, negative is worse), and the anomaly correlation coefficient (ACC): the correlation between forecast and observed anomalies, both measured from climatology.

H = np.column_stack([np.ones(n)] + [f(k * w) for k in (1, 2, 3) for f in (np.sin, np.cos)])
clim_hat = H @ np.linalg.lstsq(H[train], temp[train], rcond=None)[0]       # harmonic climatology from training years
anom = temp - clim_hat
LEADS = np.arange(1, 11)
idx_tr = np.where(train)[0]; idx_tr = idx_tr[(idx_tr >= 2) & (idx_tr + 10 < n)]
idx_te = np.where(test)[0]; idx_te = idx_te[(idx_te >= 2) & (idx_te + 10 < n)]
lags = lambda i: np.c_[anom[i], anom[i - 1], anom[i - 2]]

scores, ar_models, resid_sd = {k: {} for k in ["persistence", "climatology", "AR model"]}, {}, {}
for L in LEADS:
    ar_models[L] = LinearRegression().fit(lags(idx_tr), anom[idx_tr + L])
    resid_sd[L] = np.std(anom[idx_tr + L] - ar_models[L].predict(lags(idx_tr)))
    obs = temp[idx_te + L]
    fc = {"persistence": temp[idx_te], "climatology": clim_hat[idx_te + L],
          "AR model": clim_hat[idx_te + L] + ar_models[L].predict(lags(idx_te))}
    for k, f in fc.items():
        acc = np.corrcoef(f - clim_hat[idx_te + L], obs - clim_hat[idx_te + L])[0, 1] if k != "climatology" else np.nan
        scores[k][L] = (np.sqrt(np.mean((f - obs) ** 2)), acc)
rm = {k: np.array([scores[k][L][0] for L in LEADS]) for k in scores}
print("lead (days):      " + " ".join(f"{L:6d}" for L in LEADS))
for k in scores:
    print(f"RMSE {k:12s} " + " ".join(f"{v:6.2f}" for v in rm[k]))
ss_clim = {k: 1 - rm[k] ** 2 / rm["climatology"] ** 2 for k in ["persistence", "AR model"]}
for k, v in ss_clim.items():
    print(f"skill vs climatology, {k:11s} " + " ".join(f"{x:6.2f}" for x in v))
print(f"AR skill vs persistence at day 1: {1 - rm['AR model'][0] ** 2 / rm['persistence'][0] ** 2:.2f}; "
      f"persistence becomes worse than climatology from day {LEADS[np.argmax(ss_clim['persistence'] < 0)]}")
print("ACC, AR model:    " + " ".join(f"{scores['AR model'][L][1]:6.2f}" for L in LEADS))
print("ACC, persistence: " + " ".join(f"{scores['persistence'][L][1]:6.2f}" for L in LEADS))
raw_r = np.corrcoef(clim_hat[idx_te + 5], temp[idx_te + 5])[0, 1]
print(f"plain correlation of the climatology forecast with observed temperature at day 5: {raw_r:.2f} "
      f"(the seasonal cycle alone; its anomaly correlation is zero by construction)")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.2))
for k, st in [("persistence", "o-"), ("climatology", "s--"), ("AR model", "^-")]:
    ax[0].plot(LEADS, rm[k], st, ms=4, label=k)
ax[0].set_xlabel("lead time (days)"); ax[0].set_ylabel("RMSE (deg C)"); ax[0].set_title("error against lead time", fontsize=10); ax[0].legend(fontsize=8)
for k, st in [("persistence", "o-"), ("AR model", "^-")]:
    ax[1].plot(LEADS, ss_clim[k], st, ms=4, label=k)
ax[1].axhline(0, color="k", lw=0.8); ax[1].set_xlabel("lead time (days)"); ax[1].set_ylabel("skill score vs climatology")
ax[1].set_title("skill: 1 - MSE / MSE(climatology)", fontsize=10); ax[1].legend(fontsize=8)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

3. Ensembles and the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)¶

A probabilistic forecast gives a distribution, not one number. Our AR model's errors are close to Gaussian, so a natural forecast at lead $L$ is $N(\mu, \sigma_L^2)$, with $\mu$ the AR forecast and $\sigma_L$ the standard deviation of its training errors. Weather centres instead run an ensemble: many forecasts from slightly different starting states; we imitate one by drawing members from the Gaussian.

The CRPS compares the forecast's cumulative distribution $F$ with the observation $y$: $\mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) = \int (F(x) - \mathbf{1}\{x \ge y\})^2\,dx$. It equals $E|X - y| - \tfrac12 E|X - X'|$ for independent draws $X, X'$ from $F$ (Gneiting & Raftery 2007), so it can be computed from ensemble members; for a single-number forecast it reduces to the absolute error. For a Gaussian there is a closed form: with $z = (y - \mu)/\sigma$,

$$\mathrm{CRPS} = \sigma\left[z\,(2\Phi(z) - 1) + 2\varphi(z) - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\right].$$

Lower is better. We compare a well-spread ensemble with an overconfident one (half the spread), an underconfident one (double the spread) and the climatological distribution.

def crps_gauss(mu, sd, y):
    z = (y - mu) / sd
    return sd * (z * (2 * stats.norm.cdf(z) - 1) + 2 * stats.norm.pdf(z) - 1 / np.sqrt(np.pi))

def crps_members(X, y):
    # X: (cases, members). E|X - y| - 0.5 E|X - X'|, the mean over all member pairs computed from sorted members
    m = X.shape[1]
    Xs, i = np.sort(X, axis=1), np.arange(1, m + 1)
    pair_mean = 2.0 / m ** 2 * (Xs * (2 * i - m - 1)).sum(1)
    return np.abs(X - y[:, None]).mean(1) - 0.5 * pair_mean

L = 3
mu = clim_hat[idx_te + L] + ar_models[L].predict(lags(idx_te)); y_obs = temp[idx_te + L]; s = resid_sd[L]
clim_sd = np.std(anom[train])
crps = {"AR ensemble (spread = error sd)": crps_gauss(mu, s, y_obs).mean(),
        "overconfident (half the spread)": crps_gauss(mu, 0.5 * s, y_obs).mean(),
        "underconfident (double the spread)": crps_gauss(mu, 2 * s, y_obs).mean(),
        "climatology N(clim, anomaly sd)": crps_gauss(clim_hat[idx_te + L], clim_sd, y_obs).mean(),
        "AR single number (CRPS = MAE)": np.abs(mu - y_obs).mean()}
print(f"lead {L} days, error sd of the AR model {s:.2f} C:")
for k, v in crps.items():
    print(f"  {k:36s} CRPS {v:.3f} C")
print(f"CRPS skill score of the AR ensemble vs climatology: {1 - crps['AR ensemble (spread = error sd)'] / crps['climatology N(clim, anomaly sd)']:.2f}")

rs = np.random.default_rng(1)
exact = crps_gauss(mu, s, y_obs).mean()
ms, est = [5, 10, 20, 50, 200], []
for m in ms:
    est.append(crps_members(mu[:, None] + s * rs.standard_normal((len(mu), m)), y_obs).mean())
print("members: " + ", ".join(f"m={m}: {e:.3f}" for m, e in zip(ms, est)) + f"; closed form {exact:.3f}")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.2))
ax[0].semilogx(ms, est, "o-", label="estimate from m members"); ax[0].axhline(exact, color="k", ls="--", label="closed form")
ax[0].set_xlabel("ensemble members m"); ax[0].set_ylabel("mean CRPS (deg C)"); ax[0].set_title("CRPS from members converges", fontsize=10)
ax[0].legend(fontsize=8)
names = list(crps)
ax[1].barh(range(len(names)), [crps[k] for k in names], color=["C2", "C3", "C1", "C7", "C0"])
ax[1].set_yticks(range(len(names))); ax[1].set_yticklabels([k.split(" (")[0] for k in names], fontsize=8)
ax[1].set_xlabel("mean CRPS (deg C), lower is better"); ax[1].set_title(f"lead {L} days", fontsize=10)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

4. Will it rain tomorrow? Brier score and reliability¶

A rain forecast is a probability $p$; the outcome $o$ is 1 (rain, here more than 0 mm) or 0. The Brier score is the mean of $(p - o)^2$: 0 is perfect, and lower is better. The Brier skill score compares it with climatology, $\mathrm{BSS} = 1 - \mathrm{BS}/\mathrm{BS}_{\text{clim}}$. A reliability diagram bins the forecasts by probability and plots how often it actually rained in each bin: a reliable forecast lies on the diagonal ("when I say 70%, it rains 70% of the time"). Three forecasts for tomorrow:

  • climatology: the fraction of wet days within 15 days of the same date in the training years;
  • logistic regression on today's rain (yes/no and amount) and the season (sines and cosines of the day of year);
  • overconfident: the same logistic model with its log-odds doubled, so probabilities are pushed towards 0 and 1.
o = wet.astype(int)
circ = lambda d: np.minimum(np.abs(d), 365 - np.abs(d))
clim_p = np.array([o[train][circ(doy[train] - d) <= 15].mean() for d in range(1, 367)])
Xr = lambda i: np.c_[wet[i], np.log1p(rain[i]), np.sin(w[i + 1]), np.cos(w[i + 1]), np.sin(2 * w[i + 1]), np.cos(2 * w[i + 1])]
i_tr = np.where(train)[0][:-1]; i_te = np.where(test)[0][:-1]
logit = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(Xr(i_tr), o[i_tr + 1])
p_log = logit.predict_proba(Xr(i_te))[:, 1]
z = np.log(p_log / (1 - p_log))
probs = {"climatology": clim_p[doy[i_te + 1] - 1], "logistic regression": p_log, "overconfident": 1 / (1 + np.exp(-2 * z))}
o_te = o[i_te + 1]
bs = {k: np.mean((p - o_te) ** 2) for k, p in probs.items()}
for k, p in probs.items():
    print(f"{k:20s} Brier score {bs[k]:.4f}, Brier skill score {1 - bs[k] / bs['climatology']:+.3f}, "
          f"share of forecasts below 0.1 or above 0.9: {np.mean((p < 0.1) | (p > 0.9)):.2f}")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.4))
ax[0].plot([0, 1], [0, 1], "k:", lw=1, label="perfect reliability")
for k, st in [("climatology", "s--"), ("logistic regression", "o-"), ("overconfident", "^-")]:

5. Gridded weather data¶

Station records are points. Gridded datasets cover the whole map: reanalysis combines past observations with a weather model to reconstruct the atmosphere everywhere, hour by hour. ERA5, from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), is the most used (Hersbach et al. 2020). Gridded data come as NetCDF files (one file holds labelled arrays, such as temperature by time, latitude and longitude) or as Zarr stores (the same idea split into many small chunks, so cloud storage can serve just the part you need). In Python, xarray reads both and keeps the labels.

What to notice¶

(Written from the run above; your numbers will match if you keep the seeds.)

  1. Persistence is hard to beat at one day and useless by day 5. One day ahead, persistence had an RMSE of 0.80 °C against 2.04 for climatology (skill 0.84). Its skill fell below zero at day 5 (−0.03) and reached −0.66 at day 10, while climatology stayed at 2.03 to 2.04 °C at every lead.
  2. Beating persistence at day 1 said little. The AR model beat persistence by only 12% in mean squared error at day 1. Its value showed at longer leads, where persistence fails: its skill against climatology fell from 0.86 at day 1 to 0.26 at day 5 and 0.06 at day 10, approaching climatology as a good forecast should. Report skill against both baselines at every lead.
  3. Correlation must be computed on anomalies. The climatology forecast correlated 0.89 with the observed temperature at day 5, only because both follow the seasonal cycle; its anomaly correlation is zero. The AR model's anomaly correlation fell from 0.93 at day 1 to 0.50 at day 5 and 0.22 at day 10 (persistence 0.92, 0.48 and 0.18).
  4. The CRPS rewards the right spread. At a lead of 3 days, the AR ensemble whose spread matched its error standard deviation (1.45 °C) scored 0.800; half the spread scored 0.849, double the spread 0.945, and climatology 1.133. The single-number forecast scored its mean absolute error, 1.109: giving a distribution instead of one number lowered the score by 28%. The CRPS skill against climatology was 0.29.
  5. Small ensembles overstate the CRPS. The estimate from 5 members was 0.958, from 20 members 0.843 and from 200 members 0.805, approaching the closed form, 0.800. Compare ensembles of the same size, or use an estimator corrected for ensemble size.
  6. Rain is harder, and overconfidence costs all the skill. The logistic forecast scored a Brier score of 0.1199 against 0.1310 for climatology (skill 0.085). Doubling its log-odds made 76% of its forecasts extreme (below 0.1 or above 0.9, against 52%) and erased the skill (0.1314, skill −0.003); in the reliability diagram its confident rain forecasts came true less often than they claimed.
