1. Synthetic district yields, 2001–2024, and one hot year¶

District yields come from surveys and crop-cut experiments: officials harvest small sample plots and weigh the grain. They are noisy, and they arrive once a year. The features are seasonal summaries of weather and satellite data:

  • growing degree days (GDD): the sum over the season of daily mean temperature above a base temperature, a measure of heat available for growth;
  • heat days: days above a high-temperature threshold around flowering, when heat can make rice florets sterile;
  • seasonal rainfall (mm): too little and too much both hurt;
  • peak NDVI from satellites (session 3), which follows canopy health (soil, water stress) but not floret sterility;
  • year, for the trend from new varieties and practices.

We build 64 districts × 24 years (1,536 rows) from these, with a hidden district effect, a hidden year shock (pests, prices) and survey noise. Yield falls by 0.12 t/ha for each heat day. Then we add 2025, an unusually hot year: its heat days lie far above anything in 2001–2024. All numbers are made-up assumptions for teaching.

import os
os.environ["OMP_NUM_THREADS"] = "1"     # one thread for the tree models: faster on small data, and reproducible
import time
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, QuantileRegressor
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, LeaveOneGroupOut
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

t_start = time.time()
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
G, YEARS, HOT = 8, np.arange(2001, 2025), 2025
nD = G * G
row, col = np.divmod(np.arange(nD), G)

def smooth_field(sigma=1.5):
    f = gaussian_filter(rng.normal(size=(G, G)), sigma, mode="reflect").ravel()
    return (f - f.mean()) / f.std()

soil, u_dist = smooth_field(), 0.25 * smooth_field(1.2)
heat_clim = 1.2 * (0.5 - col / (G - 1)) * 2                     # the west is hotter
rain_clim = 900 * (1 + 0.15 * (col / (G - 1) - 0.5) * 2)         # the east is wetter

def season(yr, temp_anom):
    t = yr - YEARS[0]
    gdd = 1800 + 60 * temp_anom + 40 * heat_clim + rng.normal(0, 25, nD)
    heat = np.clip(np.round(2.5 + 1.2 * heat_clim + 2.0 * temp_anom + rng.normal(0, 1.0, nD)), 0, None)
    rain = rain_clim * np.exp(0.22 * rng.normal() + 0.15 * smooth_field(2.0))
    stress = ((rain - 900) / 300) ** 2
    ndvi = 0.72 + 0.04 * soil - 0.05 * stress + rng.normal(0, 0.015, nD)            # NDVI does not see heat
    shock = 0.15 * rng.normal() + 0.08 * smooth_field(2.0)
    y = (3.6 + 0.035 * t + 0.20 * soil + u_dist - 0.12 * heat - 0.45 * stress + 0.0006 * (gdd - 1800)
         + shock + rng.normal(0, 0.12, nD))
    return pd.DataFrame({"district": np.arange(nD), "year": yr, "gdd": gdd, "heat_days": heat, "rain": rain,
                         "ndvi": ndvi, "yield": y})

df = pd.concat([season(yr, rng.normal(0, 0.6)) for yr in YEARS], ignore_index=True)
hot = season(HOT, 5.5)                                           # a temperature anomaly far outside 2001-2024
print(f"training panel {df.shape}; hot year {hot.shape}")
print(f"heat days 2001-2024: mean {df.heat_days.mean():.1f}, max {df.heat_days.max():.0f}; "
      f"hot year 2025: mean {hot.heat_days.mean():.1f}, min {hot.heat_days.min():.0f}, max {hot.heat_days.max():.0f}")
print(f"yield 2001-2024: mean {df['yield'].mean():.2f} t/ha (sd {df['yield'].std():.2f}); hot year mean {hot['yield'].mean():.2f}")
print(f"peak NDVI 2001-2024 mean {df.ndvi.mean():.3f}; hot year {hot.ndvi.mean():.3f}  (NDVI misses the heat)")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.3))
ym = df.groupby("year")["yield"].mean()
ax[0].plot(ym.index, ym.values, "o-", label="2001-2024")
ax[0].plot([HOT], [hot["yield"].mean()], "rs", ms=8, label="2025 (hot)")
ax[0].set_xlabel("year"); ax[0].set_ylabel("mean yield (t/ha)"); ax[0].set_title("district mean yield", fontsize=10)
ax[0].legend(fontsize=8)
ax[1].scatter(df.heat_days + rng.uniform(-0.2, 0.2, len(df)), df["yield"], s=4, alpha=0.4, label="2001-2024")
ax[1].scatter(hot.heat_days, hot["yield"], s=10, color="r", label="2025 (hot)")
ax[1].set_xlabel("heat days"); ax[1].set_ylabel("yield (t/ha)"); ax[1].set_title("yield against heat days", fontsize=10)
ax[1].legend(fontsize=8)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

2. Two models, two kinds of cross-validation¶

  • Linear regression on engineered features: year, GDD, heat days, rainfall and rainfall squared (so it can bend: too little and too much rain), and NDVI. Its coefficients can be read and checked against agronomy.
  • Gradient boosting (HistGradientBoostingRegressor) on the raw features: year, GDD, heat days, rainfall, NDVI. It finds bends and interactions by itself.

Random 5-fold cross-validation mixes years; leave-one-year-out holds out a whole year, which is how a yield forecast is used (session 1).

def lin_X(d):
    return np.c_[d.year, d.gdd, d.heat_days, d.rain, d.rain ** 2, d.ndvi]
def gbm_X(d):
    return np.c_[d.year, d.gdd, d.heat_days, d.rain, d.ndvi]
def make_lin():
    return make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LinearRegression())     # scaling matters: rain^2 is about 10^6
def make_gbm(**kw):
    return HistGradientBoostingRegressor(max_iter=300, learning_rate=0.05, random_state=0, **kw)

y_all = df["yield"].to_numpy()
rmse = lambda p, y: float(np.sqrt(np.mean((p - y) ** 2)))
res = {}
for split_name, sp, g in [("random 5-fold", KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0), None),
                          ("leave-one-year-out", LeaveOneGroupOut(), df.year.to_numpy())]:
    for model_name, mk, fx in [("linear", make_lin, lin_X), ("boosting", make_gbm, gbm_X)]:
        X, pred = fx(df), np.zeros(len(df))
        for tr, te in sp.split(X, y_all, g):
            pred[te] = mk().fit(X[tr], y_all[tr]).predict(X[te])
        res[split_name, model_name] = rmse(pred, y_all)
        print(f"{split_name:20s} {model_name:8s} RMSE {res[split_name, model_name]:.3f} t/ha")
lin_full = make_lin().fit(lin_X(df), y_all)
coef = lin_full[-1].coef_ / lin_full[0].scale_                   # back to the original units
print("linear model: t/ha per year %.4f, per heat day %.4f, per unit NDVI %.2f" % (coef[0], coef[2], coef[5]))

3. The hot year: can the model extrapolate?¶

In 2025 every district had more heat days than any district in 2001–2024. A regression tree splits the training range into boxes and predicts a constant in each, so beyond the largest heat-day value it has seen it can only repeat the prediction for the edge. A linear model with the right feature carries the slope on, which is right here because the true effect is linear. We train on all of 2001–2024 and predict 2025.

lin_all = make_lin().fit(lin_X(df), y_all)
gbm_all = make_gbm().fit(gbm_X(df), y_all)
yhot = hot["yield"].to_numpy()
hot_preds = {"linear": lin_all.predict(lin_X(hot)), "boosting": gbm_all.predict(gbm_X(hot))}
for k, p in hot_preds.items():
    print(f"hot year 2025, {k:8s} RMSE {rmse(p, yhot):.3f}, mean error {np.mean(p - yhot):+.3f} t/ha")
print(f"true mean yield 2025: {yhot.mean():.2f} t/ha")

# partial dependence: vary heat days, keep the other features at their 2024 medians
base = df[df.year == 2024].median(numeric_only=True)
grid = pd.DataFrame({"year": 2024, "gdd": base.gdd, "heat_days": np.arange(0, 21), "rain": base.rain, "ndvi": base.ndvi})
plt.figure(figsize=(7, 3.3))
plt.axvspan(df.heat_days.min(), df.heat_days.max(), color="0.9", label="training range")
plt.plot(grid.heat_days, lin_all.predict(lin_X(grid)), "C0-", label="linear")
plt.plot(grid.heat_days, gbm_all.predict(gbm_X(grid)), "C1--", label="boosting")
plt.scatter(hot.heat_days, yhot, s=10, color="r", label="2025 districts (truth)")
plt.xlabel("heat days"); plt.ylabel("predicted yield (t/ha)"); plt.title("predictions against heat days, other features fixed", fontsize=10)
plt.legend(fontsize=8); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

4. Prediction intervals with quantile regression¶

A single number hides the uncertainty. Quantile regression fits the $q$-th quantile directly by minimizing the pinball loss: for a residual $r = y - \hat y_q$, the loss is $q\,r$ if $r \ge 0$ and $(q - 1)\,r$ if $r < 0$. Fitting $q = 0.05$ and $q = 0.95$ gives a 90% prediction interval. We check two things on data the models never saw: coverage (the share of true yields inside the interval; it should be near 0.90) and width. Normal years are tested by leave-one-year-out over all 24 years; then we train on 2001–2024 and test the hot year. Course 2 session 7 covers intervals for estimates; these are intervals for single predictions.

def quantile_models(Xtr, ytr, kind):
    if kind == "linear":
        return [make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), QuantileRegressor(quantile=q, alpha=0.0, solver="highs")).fit(Xtr, ytr)
                for q in (0.05, 0.95)]
    return [HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile", quantile=q, max_iter=200, learning_rate=0.05, random_state=0)
            .fit(Xtr, ytr) for q in (0.05, 0.95)]

def check(models, X, y):
    lo, hi = models[0].predict(X), models[1].predict(X)
    return float(np.mean((y >= lo) & (y <= hi))), float(np.mean(hi - lo)), lo, hi

intervals = {}
for kind, fx in [("linear", lin_X), ("boosting", gbm_X)]:
    inside, width = np.zeros(len(df), bool), np.zeros(len(df))
    for yr in YEARS:                                           # leave-one-year-out intervals
        tr, te = df.year != yr, df.year == yr
        ins, w, lo_, hi_ = check(quantile_models(fx(df[tr]), y_all[tr], kind), fx(df[te]), y_all[te])
        inside[te.to_numpy()] = (y_all[te] >= lo_) & (y_all[te] <= hi_); width[te.to_numpy()] = hi_ - lo_
    per_year = pd.Series(inside).groupby(df.year.to_numpy()).mean()
    cov_h, w_h, lo, hi = check(quantile_models(fx(df), y_all, kind), fx(hot), yhot)
    intervals[kind] = (lo, hi)
    print(f"{kind:8s} 90% interval, leave-one-year-out: coverage {inside.mean():.3f} (by year {per_year.min():.2f} to "
          f"{per_year.max():.2f}), width {width.mean():.2f} t/ha | hot year: coverage {cov_h:.3f}, width {w_h:.2f} t/ha")

order = np.argsort(yhot)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 3.3))
for j, (kind, c) in enumerate([("linear", "C0"), ("boosting", "C1")]):
    lo, hi = intervals[kind]
    ax.vlines(np.arange(nD) + (j - 0.5) * 0.3, lo[order], hi[order], color=c, lw=1.5, label=f"{kind} 90% interval")
ax.plot(np.arange(nD), yhot[order], "k.", label="true yield")
ax.set_xlabel("district (sorted by true yield)"); ax.set_ylabel("yield in 2025 (t/ha)")
ax.set_title("prediction intervals in the hot year", fontsize=10); ax.legend(fontsize=8)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
print(f"runtime so far {time.time() - t_start:.0f} s")

5. Real weather features (NOT RUN here)¶

Heat days and GDD come from daily temperatures. The cell below downloads hourly 2 m temperature from ERA5-Land (Copernicus Climate Data Store) for a box around one district for one season, and computes daily maximum and mean temperature, heat days and GDD. If the request fails, copy the current request format from the dataset's download form on the CDS website. For remote-sensing yield models that learn their own features from satellite images, see You et al. (2017).

# NOT RUN: needs a free Copernicus Climate Data Store account with an API key in ~/.cdsapirc, and pip install cdsapi xarray netCDF4
import cdsapi
import xarray as xr

cdsapi.Client().retrieve("reanalysis-era5-land", {
    "variable": ["2m_temperature"],
    "year": ["2024"], "month": ["02", "03", "04"],
    "day": [f"{d:02d}" for d in range(1, 32)], "time": [f"{h:02d}:00" for h in range(24)],
    "area": [25.0, 89.2, 24.7, 89.5],                    # north, west, south, east: a small box (example: around Bogura)
    "data_format": "netcdf", "download_format": "unarchived",
}, "era5land_t2m_2024.nc")
ds = xr.open_dataset("era5land_t2m_2024.nc")
tname = "valid_time" if "valid_time" in ds.coords else "time"
t2m = (ds["t2m"] - 273.15).mean(["latitude", "longitude"])          # box mean, deg C (UTC hours; shift by +6 h for local days)
tmax = t2m.resample({tname: "1D"}).max(); tmean = t2m.resample({tname: "1D"}).mean()
heat_days = int((tmax > 35).sum())                                    # threshold: choose it from agronomy for your crop
gdd = float((tmean - 10).clip(min=0).sum())                           # base temperature 10 deg C (crop-specific)
print(f"heat days {heat_days}, GDD {gdd:.0f}")

What to notice¶

(Written from the run above; your numbers will match if you keep the seeds.)

  1. Random cross-validation flattered the flexible model. Under random 5-fold cross-validation both models scored RMSE 0.323 t/ha. Leaving out whole years, the linear model stayed close (0.334) while boosting rose to 0.437: it had learned each year's hidden shock from the other districts of that year, through the year feature, which no real forecast can do.
  2. The linear model recovered the agronomy. It estimated −0.1088 t/ha per heat day (true −0.12) and +0.0290 t/ha per year (true 0.035), and a coefficient of 5.90 per unit of NDVI, which carries the soil and water-stress information. Its features were standardized first: rainfall squared is about $10^6$, and badly scaled features can make a least-squares solver lose small but real coefficients.
  3. Trees could not extrapolate to the hot year. Every district had 10 to 17 heat days in 2025, above the training maximum of 8. Boosting predicted as if heat stopped mattering at the edge of its training range: mean error +0.919 t/ha (RMSE 0.989) against a true mean of 2.91 t/ha. The linear model, whose heat slope continues, was almost unbiased (mean error −0.016, RMSE 0.269). Extrapolation works here only because the true effect really is linear; with a wrong functional form a linear model can fail just as badly.
  4. NDVI did not warn about the heat. Peak NDVI in 2025 was 0.700, slightly above the 2001–2024 mean of 0.683, because in this synthetic world heat sterility does not change greenness.
  5. Intervals need honest testing. Linear quantile regression's 90% intervals covered 0.876 of yields in held-out years (between 0.69 and 0.97 in single years, because a year shock moves all districts together) and 0.938 in the hot year. Boosting's quantile intervals were narrower (0.74 t/ha) and covered only 0.671 in held-out years and 0.266 in the hot year, probably because they had fitted the training years' shocks and so were too sure about a new year. Against expectation, even the linear intervals fell short of 0.90 in held-out years.
