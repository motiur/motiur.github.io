1. A synthetic year of satellite images¶

Sentinel-2 measures sunlight reflected from the ground in 13 spectral bands. We simulate five of them, at their band centres: blue (B2, 490 nm), green (B3, 560 nm), red (B4, 665 nm), near-infrared or NIR (B8, 842 nm) and short-wave infrared or SWIR (B11, 1,610 nm). Values are surface reflectance, the fraction of light reflected (0 to 1).

Each surface has a typical spectral signature (the numbers below are rough, typical values):

  • green vegetation absorbs red light for photosynthesis and reflects NIR strongly;
  • water is dark, darkest in NIR and SWIR;
  • bare soil gets brighter from blue to SWIR;
  • built-up areas are moderately bright in every band;
  • clouds are bright in every band, including blue.

The scene is 48 × 48 pixels (10 m pixels, so about 0.5 km across) cut into rectangular fields of five classes: Boro rice (the dry-season, mainly irrigated crop), Aman rice (the monsoon crop), water, built-up and bare soil. A rice field is flooded around transplanting, then the canopy covers the water and soil, peaks and is harvested. We sample the year every 5 days, like Sentinel-2's revisit time at the equator with two satellites: 73 dates.

The crop calendars, field sizes and cloud amounts here are illustrative assumptions, not measurements. The real data this stands in for is a Sentinel-2 Level-2A time series (section 7).

import time
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter, binary_dilation, shift as nd_shift
from scipy.signal import savgol_filter

t_start = time.time()
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
H = W = 48
DOY = np.arange(1, 366, 5)                          # day of year of the 73 acquisitions
T = len(DOY)
BANDS, WAVE = ["blue", "green", "red", "nir", "swir"], [490, 560, 665, 842, 1610]
REFL = {"vegetation": [0.03, 0.08, 0.04, 0.42, 0.20], "soil": [0.09, 0.13, 0.17, 0.25, 0.32],
        "water": [0.07, 0.08, 0.06, 0.03, 0.01], "built-up": [0.12, 0.13, 0.15, 0.20, 0.24],
        "cloud": [0.45, 0.45, 0.47, 0.50, 0.35]}
R = {k: np.array(v) for k, v in REFL.items()}
CLASSES = ["Boro rice", "Aman rice", "water", "built-up", "bare soil"]

# fields of 6 x 8 pixels, each with one class
field_id = (np.arange(H)[:, None] // 6) * (W // 8) + (np.arange(W)[None, :] // 8)
n_fields = field_id.max() + 1
field_class = rng.choice(5, n_fields, p=[0.30, 0.34, 0.08, 0.12, 0.16])
lc = field_class[field_id]                           # land-cover map, H x W

def double_logistic(t, up, down, k=7.0):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(t - up) / k)) - 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(t - down) / k))

# per-field calendar: transplanting day, peak cover; green-up = the day canopy cover reaches half its peak
transplant = np.where(field_class == 0, rng.normal(20, 7, n_fields), rng.normal(205, 9, n_fields))
peak_cover = rng.uniform(0.82, 0.95, n_fields)
up = transplant + 28 + rng.normal(0, 3, n_fields)
down = np.where(field_class == 0, up + 68, up + 75) + rng.normal(0, 4, n_fields)

surface = np.zeros((T, H, W, 5))                     # clear-sky surface reflectance
for f in range(n_fields):
    pix = field_id == f
    c = field_class[f]
    if c in (0, 1):
        cover = peak_cover[f] * double_logistic(DOY, up[f], down[f])
        flood = 0.8 * ((DOY > transplant[f] - 15) & (DOY < transplant[f] + 25)) * (1 - cover)
        spec = cover[:, None] * R["vegetation"] + flood[:, None] * R["water"] + (1 - cover - flood)[:, None] * R["soil"]
    else:
        spec = np.tile(R[["", "", "water", "built-up", "soil"][c]], (T, 1))
    surface[:, pix, :] = spec[:, None, :]
surface = np.clip(surface + rng.normal(0, 0.006, surface.shape), 0.001, 1)    # pixel-to-pixel variation

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.3))
for k, v in REFL.items():
    ax[0].plot(WAVE, v, "o-", label=k)
ax[0].set_xlabel("wavelength (nm)"); ax[0].set_ylabel("reflectance"); ax[0].set_title("typical spectral signatures", fontsize=10)
ax[0].legend(fontsize=8)
im = ax[1].imshow(lc, cmap=plt.matplotlib.colors.ListedColormap(["#4daf4a", "#a6d96a", "#377eb8", "#999999", "#d8b365"]))
cb = plt.colorbar(im, ax=ax[1], ticks=np.arange(5) * 0.8 + 0.4); cb.ax.set_yticklabels(CLASSES, fontsize=8)

2. Clouds, shadows and two indices¶

Now the sky. Each date gets a random cloud field: thick cloud cores with thin edges, and a shadow displaced from each cloud by the sun's angle. In the monsoon there is also a thin, wide haze that lowers contrast without looking like a cloud. In our synthetic year cloud covers about a tenth of the scene in the dry season and more than half in the monsoon, an assumption that matches the familiar problem: the monsoon crop grows under cloudy skies.

Two normalized difference indices summarize the bands:

$$\mathrm{NDVI} = \frac{\mathrm{NIR} - \mathrm{Red}}{\mathrm{NIR} + \mathrm{Red}} \;\text{(Tucker 1979)}, \qquad \mathrm{NDWI} = \frac{\mathrm{Green} - \mathrm{NIR}}{\mathrm{Green} + \mathrm{NIR}} \;\text{(McFeeters 1996)}.$$

Dense green vegetation gives NDVI of about 0.7 to 0.8; open water gives NDWI above 0. A flooded rice field just after transplanting is water-like; a closed rice canopy is vegetation-like.

cloud_frac = 0.10 + 0.65 * np.exp(-((DOY - 215) / 45.0) ** 2)    # assumed seasonal cloud fraction
alpha = np.zeros((T, H, W)); shadow = np.zeros((T, H, W), bool)
for t in range(T):
    g = gaussian_filter(rng.normal(size=(H, W)), 4)
    thr = np.quantile(g, 1 - cloud_frac[t])
    alpha[t] = np.clip((g - thr) / (0.6 * g.std()), 0, 1)          # cloud opacity: thin edges, thick cores
    shadow[t] = (nd_shift((alpha[t] > 0.3).astype(float), (5, 4), order=0, mode="constant") > 0.5) & (alpha[t] < 0.05)
    hz = gaussian_filter(rng.normal(size=(H, W)), 8)                # thin haze: low opacity, wide, hard to see
    hz = (hz - hz.min()) / (hz.max() - hz.min())
    haze_level = 0.15 * np.exp(-((DOY[t] - 215) / 50.0) ** 2) * rng.uniform(0.3, 1.0)
    alpha[t] = 1 - (1 - alpha[t]) * (1 - haze_level * hz)
obs = surface * np.where(shadow, 0.35, 1.0)[..., None]
obs = (1 - alpha[..., None]) * obs + alpha[..., None] * R["cloud"] + rng.normal(0, 0.004, obs.shape)

ndvi = lambda x: (x[..., 3] - x[..., 2]) / (x[..., 3] + x[..., 2] + 1e-9)
ndwi = lambda x: (x[..., 1] - x[..., 3]) / (x[..., 1] + x[..., 3] + 1e-9)
NDVI_true, NDVI_obs, NDWI_true = ndvi(surface), ndvi(obs), ndwi(surface)

t_boro = int(np.argmin(np.abs(DOY - 76)))             # mid-March: Boro near its peak
t_flood = int(np.argmin(np.abs(DOY - 206)))           # late July: Aman fields flooded for transplanting
print(f"clear-sky indices by class on day {DOY[t_boro]} and day {DOY[t_flood]}:")
for k in range(5):
    m = lc == k
    print(f"  {CLASSES[k]:10s} NDVI {NDVI_true[t_boro][m].mean():5.2f} {NDVI_true[t_flood][m].mean():5.2f}   "
          f"NDWI {NDWI_true[t_boro][m].mean():5.2f} {NDWI_true[t_flood][m].mean():5.2f}")
print(f"cloudy fraction of the scene (opacity > 0.05): mean {np.mean(alpha > 0.05):.2f}; "
      f"in July-September {np.mean(alpha[(DOY >= 182) & (DOY <= 273)] > 0.05):.2f}; "
      f"in December-February {np.mean(alpha[(DOY <= 59) | (DOY >= 335)] > 0.05):.2f}")

3. A cloud mask from the blue band¶

Clouds are bright in blue, and almost nothing on the ground is. A simple rule flags a pixel as cloud when its blue reflectance is above 0.15, grows the mask by one pixel to catch thin edges, and projects it by the sun's offset to flag shadows. Real masks (the Sentinel-2 scene classification layer, Fmask, s2cloudless) use more bands and machine learning, but the idea is the same. We measure the mask against the truth: recall is the share of contaminated pixels it catches, precision the share of flagged pixels that really were contaminated.

blue_flag = obs[..., 0] > 0.15
mask = np.zeros_like(blue_flag)
for t in range(T):
    cl = binary_dilation(blue_flag[t], iterations=1)
    mask[t] = cl | (nd_shift(cl.astype(float), (5, 4), order=0, mode="constant") > 0.5)
contaminated = (alpha > 0.03) | shadow
tp = (mask & contaminated).sum()
print(f"cloud-and-shadow mask: precision {tp / mask.sum():.3f}, recall {tp / contaminated.sum():.3f}")
missed = contaminated & ~mask
err_missed = np.abs(NDVI_obs - NDVI_true)[missed]
print(f"missed contaminated pixels: {missed.mean():.3f} of all pixel-dates; their mean NDVI error {err_missed.mean():.3f} "
      f"(clear pixels: {np.abs(NDVI_obs - NDVI_true)[~contaminated].mean():.3f})")

t_show = int(np.argmin(np.abs(DOY - 236)))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(9, 2.6))
rgb = np.clip(obs[t_show][..., [2, 1, 0]] * 3.0, 0, 1)
ax[0].imshow(rgb); ax[0].set_title(f"true colour, day {DOY[t_show]}", fontsize=9)
ax[1].imshow(NDVI_obs[t_show], cmap="RdYlGn", vmin=-0.2, vmax=0.9); ax[1].set_title("observed NDVI", fontsize=9)
ax[2].imshow(contaminated[t_show], cmap="gray"); ax[2].set_title("true cloud + shadow", fontsize=9)
ax[3].imshow(mask[t_show], cmap="gray"); ax[3].set_title("blue-band mask", fontsize=9)
for a in ax:
    a.set_xticks([]); a.set_yticks([])
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

4. Composites: one date or many?¶

A composite combines several dates into one cloud-free image. We compare three for a monsoon month (days 216 to 241, six acquisitions) against the true clear-sky NDVI averaged over that window:

  • best single date: the date with the fewest masked pixels;
  • median composite: for each pixel, the median NDVI of its unmasked dates;
  • maximum-NDVI composite: for each pixel, the largest NDVI over all dates, with no mask at all, because clouds lower NDVI.
win = np.where((DOY >= 214) & (DOY <= 243))[0]
truth = NDVI_true[win].mean(0)
best = win[np.argmin(mask[win].sum(axis=(1, 2)))]
single = np.where(mask[best], np.nan, NDVI_obs[best])
masked = np.where(mask[win], np.nan, NDVI_obs[win])
median_comp = np.nanmedian(np.where(np.all(np.isnan(masked), 0), 0, masked), axis=0)
median_comp[np.all(np.isnan(masked), 0)] = np.nan
max_comp = NDVI_obs[win].max(0)
rmse = lambda a: np.sqrt(np.nanmean((a - truth) ** 2))
gap = lambda a: np.mean(np.isnan(a))
print(f"window days {DOY[win].tolist()}; masked share per date {np.round(mask[win].mean(axis=(1, 2)), 2).tolist()}")
print(f"best single date (day {DOY[best]}): RMSE {rmse(single):.3f}, pixels with no value {gap(single):.3f}")
print(f"median composite:              RMSE {rmse(median_comp):.3f}, pixels with no value {gap(median_comp):.3f}")
print(f"maximum-NDVI composite:        RMSE {rmse(max_comp):.3f}, mean bias {np.mean(max_comp - truth):+.3f}")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(9, 2.6))
for a, img, ttl in zip(ax, [single, median_comp, max_comp, truth],

    a.imshow(np.ma.masked_invalid(img), cmap="RdYlGn", vmin=-0.2, vmax=0.9); a.set_title(ttl, fontsize=9)
    a.set_xticks([]); a.set_yticks([])
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

5. Smoothing a cloudy NDVI time series¶

A crop's NDVI over the season traces its phenology: green-up after transplanting, a peak, then senescence and harvest. Clouds that slip through the mask pull NDVI down, so raw series are jagged. The Savitzky–Golay filter (Savitzky & Golay 1964) fits a low-order polynomial in a sliding window and keeps the shape of peaks better than a moving average (scipy.signal.savgol_filter; here 7 dates = 35 days, polynomial order 2). We compare four series for each pixel:

  1. raw + SG: smooth the raw series, clouds and all;
  2. masked + interpolated: drop masked dates and interpolate linearly;
  3. masked + interpolated + SG: then smooth;
  4. raw + SG upper envelope: no mask, but repeatedly replace values below the smooth curve by the curve and smooth again, a simple version of the method of Chen et al. (2004), which uses the fact that noise in NDVI is mostly downward.
def interp_masked(y, m):
    ok = ~m
    return np.interp(DOY, DOY[ok], y[ok]) if ok.sum() >= 2 else np.full_like(y, np.nan)

def sg(y):
    return savgol_filter(y, 7, 2)

def sg_upper_envelope(y, n_iter=5):
    fit, yy = sg(y), y.copy()
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        yy = np.maximum(yy, fit)
        fit = sg(yy)
    return fit

Y = NDVI_obs.reshape(T, -1).T; Mk = mask.reshape(T, -1).T; Ytrue = NDVI_true.reshape(T, -1).T
aman = np.where(lc.ravel() == 1)[0]
series = {"raw + SG": lambda i: sg(Y[i]),
          "masked + interpolated": lambda i: interp_masked(Y[i], Mk[i]),
          "masked + interpolated + SG": lambda i: sg(interp_masked(Y[i], Mk[i])),
          "raw + SG upper envelope": lambda i: sg_upper_envelope(Y[i])}

def green_up(y, lo=150, hi=365):
    # first day after the pre-season minimum when NDVI crosses halfway from that minimum to the season's peak
    sel = (DOY >= lo) & (DOY <= hi)
    d, v = DOY[sel], y[sel]
    i_pk = int(np.argmax(v)); i_mn = int(np.argmin(v[:i_pk + 1]))
    half = v[i_mn] + 0.5 * (v[i_pk] - v[i_mn])
    for j in range(i_mn, i_pk):
        if v[j] < half <= v[j + 1]:
            return d[j] + (half - v[j]) / (v[j + 1] - v[j]) * (d[j + 1] - d[j])
    return np.nan

gu_true = np.array([green_up(Ytrue[i]) for i in aman])
print(f"{len(aman)} Aman pixels; true green-up from day {np.nanmin(gu_true):.0f} to {np.nanmax(gu_true):.0f}")
errors = {}
for name, f in series.items():
    est = np.array([green_up(f(i)) for i in aman])
    rm = np.sqrt(np.mean((np.array([f(i) for i in aman]) - Ytrue[aman]) ** 2))
    errors[name] = est - gu_true
    print(f"{name:28s} NDVI RMSE {rm:.3f}; green-up error: mean {np.nanmean(errors[name]):+6.1f} days, "
          f"mean absolute {np.nanmean(np.abs(errors[name])):5.1f} days")

i0 = aman[np.argmax(Mk[aman][:, (DOY > 200) & (DOY < 260)].sum(1))]       # a cloudy Aman pixel

ax[1].axhline(0, color="k", lw=0.8); ax[1].set_ylabel("green-up error (days)"); ax[1].set_title("Aman green-up date error", fontsize=10)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

6. NDVI is not yield¶

NDVI measures green canopy, not grain. Two fields with the same NDVI curve can yield differently, for example when heat at flowering kills florets but leaves the leaves green, or when lodging or pests strike late in the season. NDVI is a useful input to yield models (session 5), not a yield measurement. Fields that differ in how they were planted can also share NDVI values on any one date: the Boro and Aman calendars only separate when you look at the whole time series, which is why session 4 classifies time series rather than single images.

7. Real Sentinel-2 data from a STAC catalogue (NOT RUN here)¶

Sentinel-2 Level-2A (surface reflectance) is free. A STAC catalogue (SpatioTemporal Asset Catalog) lets you search scenes by place, time and cloud cover, and odc-stac loads the bands as an array. Element 84's Earth Search catalogue on AWS needs no account. Its sentinel-2-c1-l2a collection stores the scale and offset that convert integers to reflectance in each asset's raster:bands metadata; read them rather than assuming them. The scene classification layer (scl) marks vegetation (4), bare soil (5) and water (6) among other classes, so keeping only those values is a quick cloud mask.

# NOT RUN: needs internet and pip install pystac-client odc-stac (reads public Sentinel-2 data on AWS; no account)
import pystac_client
import odc.stac

catalog = pystac_client.Client.open("https://earth-search.aws.element84.com/v1")
bbox = [89.33, 24.82, 89.40, 24.88]                       # lon/lat box in Bogura district, north-west Bangladesh
items = list(catalog.search(collections=["sentinel-2-c1-l2a"], bbox=bbox, datetime="2024-01-01/2024-12-31",
                            query={"eo:cloud_cover": {"lt": 80}}).items())
print(len(items), "scenes;", sorted(items[0].assets.keys())[:12])
ds = odc.stac.load(items, bands=["red", "nir", "green", "scl"], bbox=bbox, crs="EPSG:32645",
                   resolution=10, groupby="solar_day", chunks={})
rb = items[0].assets["red"].extra_fields["raster:bands"][0]
scale, offset = rb.get("scale", 1.0), rb.get("offset", 0.0)           # check these before trusting the values
refl = {b: ds[b].where(ds[b] > 0) * scale + offset for b in ["red", "nir", "green"]}
clear = ds["scl"].isin([4, 5, 6])                                      # vegetation, bare soil, water
ndvi_s2 = ((refl["nir"] - refl["red"]) / (refl["nir"] + refl["red"])).where(clear)
monthly = ndvi_s2.resample(time="1MS").median().compute()               # monthly median composites
print(monthly.mean(dim=["x", "y"]).values.round(2))

What to notice¶

(Written from the run above; your numbers will match if you keep the seeds.)

  1. One date is not enough to tell crops apart. On day 76 (mid-March), Boro fields had NDVI 0.74 while Aman fields, still bare, had 0.19, the same as bare soil. On day 206 the newly flooded Aman fields had NDWI 0.04 (water-like; open water 0.46) and NDVI 0.01, while harvested Boro fields were back at 0.19. What a field looks like depends on the date.
  2. The monsoon hides the monsoon crop. In this synthetic year, cloud and haze covered 0.63 of the scene in July to September against 0.09 in December to February, and the mask removed 78% to 91% of each date in the monsoon window.
  3. The blue-band mask caught most contamination, not all. Precision was 0.878 and recall 0.902. Thin haze slipped through: 3.3% of pixel-dates were contaminated but not flagged, with a mean NDVI error of 0.040 against 0.018 on clear pixels. Growing the mask and projecting shadows also flagged some clear pixels.
  4. Composites trade completeness against bias. For days 216 to 241, the best single date (day 236) had RMSE 0.083 where it had data but no value for 78% of pixels; the median composite had RMSE 0.087 with 41.6% missing; the maximum-NDVI composite filled every pixel but read 0.058 too high on average (RMSE 0.168), because it picks the greenest moment and the noise. Against expectation, the median composite was not more accurate than the best single date; its gain was coverage, 58% of pixels instead of 22%.
  5. Masking mattered more than smoothing. Smoothing the raw series put green-up 17.4 days late on average, because clouds pull NDVI down exactly during green-up. Masking and interpolating cut the mean absolute error to 5.0 days. Against the plan's expectation, adding Savitzky–Golay smoothing did not help further (5.2 days): the mask had already removed most bad values. The upper-envelope trick improved the unmasked series (10.1 days) but did not replace a mask. Every method was late on average (+3.8 to +17.4 days), because straight lines across cloudy gaps rise more slowly than the crop.
