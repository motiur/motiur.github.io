1. Synthetic "observed" and "model" daily series¶

A global climate model gives daily weather on a coarse grid, and even at that scale it has biases: its typical values, its spread and its number of rainy days differ from what stations or gridded observations record. Before its projections are used for a district, the biases are corrected against observations from a past period.

All series here are synthetic. For one place we draw 30 years of daily data for a historical period (1985–2014) and a future period (2071–2100):

  • Temperature, standing for the daily maximum in the hot season. The model is 1.5 °C too cool and its day-to-day spread is too small (1.8 °C against 2.5 °C). In the future the model warms by 2 °C on average and its spread grows by 25%, so its hot days warm more than its average day.
  • Rainfall. The model has far too many drizzle days (light rain on days that should be dry) and too few heavy falls. In the future its rain gets a little heavier on average and much heavier at the top.

Real data would be a CMIP6 model's daily output and a station record or gridded observations (section 6).

def rain(p_wet, shape, scale, drizzle):
    """Daily rain (mm): wet days with gamma amounts, plus light 'drizzle' (0.1-1 mm) on a share of the dry days."""
    wet = rng.random(n) < p_wet
    r = np.where(wet, rng.gamma(shape, scale, n), 0.0)
    return np.where(~wet & (rng.random(n) < drizzle), rng.uniform(0.1, 1.0, n), r)

2. Three corrections for temperature¶

  • Delta method (change factor): keep the observed series and add the model's change in the mean, $\bar x_{\text{mod,fut}} - \bar x_{\text{mod,hist}}$. It trusts the observations' shape and only the model's average change.
  • Empirical quantile mapping (QM): learn the map from the model's historical distribution to the observed one, value by value through their quantiles, $x \mapsto F_{\text{obs}}^{-1}(F_{\text{mod,hist}}(x))$, and apply the same map to the future. It corrects the whole distribution, but because the map stretches the model's narrow spread, it also stretches the model's change. A model value hotter than anything in the historical model run has no quantile to map to, so a simple QM puts it at the hottest observed value.
  • Quantile delta mapping (QDM) (Cannon et al. 2015): for each future value, find its quantile $\tau$ in the model's future distribution, compute the model's change at that quantile, $x - F_{\text{mod,hist}}^{-1}(\tau)$, and add it to the observed value at the same quantile, $F_{\text{obs}}^{-1}(\tau)$. It corrects the distribution and keeps the model's change at every quantile.

We cannot check any method against the true future, which nobody knows; we check whether each keeps the change the model projects.

def quantile_map(obs, mod_h, x, n_q=1001):
    """Empirical quantile mapping; values outside the historical model range get the end values (no extrapolation)."""
    p = np.linspace(0, 1, n_q)
    return np.interp(x, np.quantile(mod_h, p), np.quantile(obs, p))

def ecdf(sample, x):
    s = np.sort(sample)
    return np.clip((np.searchsorted(s, x, side="right") - 0.5) / len(s), 0, 1)

def qdm_additive(obs, mod_h, mod_f):
    tau = ecdf(mod_f, mod_f)                                    # quantile of each future value in the future run
    return np.quantile(obs, tau) + (mod_f - np.quantile(mod_h, tau))

t_corr = {"raw model": t_mod_f,
          "delta": t_obs + (t_mod_f.mean() - t_mod_h.mean()),
          "QM": quantile_map(t_obs, t_mod_h, t_mod_f),
          "QDM": qdm_additive(t_obs, t_mod_h, t_mod_f)}
print(f"model's own change: mean {t_mod_f.mean() - t_mod_h.mean():+.2f}, 99th percentile {q99(t_mod_f) - q99(t_mod_h):+.2f} deg C")

3. Rainfall: drizzle, extremes and multiplicative changes¶

For rain the changes are multiplicative (a 10% wetter future), so the delta method multiplies by the ratio of means and QDM multiplies by the ratio at each quantile. Dry days need care: the quantiles of the many zeros are all zero, and a ratio with zero is undefined. A common trick is to replace values under a trace amount (0.1 mm) by tiny random values before correcting, and to set corrected values under 0.1 mm back to zero afterwards. Because the model's drizzle sits in its lowest quantiles, mapping those quantiles onto the observed zeros removes the drizzle.

TRACE = 0.1
def jitter(x):
    x = x.copy(); dry = x < TRACE
    x[dry] = rng.uniform(0.001, TRACE, dry.sum())
    return x

def qm_rain(obs, mod_h, x):
    out = quantile_map(jitter(obs), jitter(mod_h), jitter(x)); out[out < TRACE] = 0.0
    return out

def qdm_multiplicative(obs, mod_h, mod_f):
    o, h, f = jitter(obs), jitter(mod_h), jitter(mod_f)
    tau = ecdf(f, f)
    out = np.quantile(o, tau) * f / np.quantile(h, tau)        # observed value times the model's ratio at that quantile
    out[out < TRACE] = 0.0
    return out

r_corr = {"raw model": r_mod_f,
          "delta": r_obs * r_mod_f.mean() / r_mod_h.mean(),
          "QM": qm_rain(r_obs, r_mod_h, r_mod_f),
          "QDM": qdm_multiplicative(r_obs, r_mod_h, r_mod_f)}
print(f"model's own change: mean {100 * (r_mod_f.mean() / r_mod_h.mean() - 1):+.1f}%, 99th percentile {100 * (q99(r_mod_f) / q99(r_mod_h) - 1):+.1f}%")
print(f"QM on the historical run: {rain_stats(qm_rain(r_obs, r_mod_h, r_mod_h))}")
print("future, corrected   rain days   mean    99th pct | change from observed: mean   99th pct")

4. Downscaling: from an 8 × 8 grid to 32 × 32¶

Bias correction fixes the statistics at one place; downscaling adds the spatial detail a coarse grid cannot show. Here a 32 × 32 region has hills, and on each day the fine rain field is a large-scale rain pattern, multiplied by a terrain factor (hills get more rain) and by small random showers that no coarse field can predict. The coarse field is the average over each 4 × 4 block, giving 8 × 8 values, as a coarse model would. There are 1,500 training days and 400 test days, plus 400 "future" days whose large-scale rain is 30% heavier.

The fields are synthetic. Real work would pair coarse model or reanalysis output with high-resolution gridded observations. The baseline is bicubic interpolation, which only smooths the coarse values onto the fine grid.

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
FINE, COARSE = 32, 8
yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:FINE, 0:FINE] / FINE
elev = (1.2 * np.exp(-((xx - 0.8) ** 2 / 0.02 + (yy - 0.25) ** 2 / 0.08))
        + 0.6 * np.exp(-((xx - 0.2) ** 2 / 0.01 + (yy - 0.8) ** 2 / 0.03))
        + 0.3 * ndimage.gaussian_filter(rng.normal(size=(FINE, FINE)), 2))
elev = np.clip(elev, 0, None)                                  # a terrain-like height map (arbitrary units)
terrain = (1 + 2.5 * elev) / (1 + 2.5 * elev).mean()          # hills get more rain

def rain_days(n, scale=1.0):
    big = ndimage.gaussian_filter(rng.normal(size=(n, FINE, FINE)), (0, 6, 6), mode="wrap")
    big /= big.std()                                           # the large-scale rain pattern
    intensity = scale * np.exp(rng.normal(1.8, 0.5, (n, 1, 1)) + 0.6 * big)
    showers = ndimage.gaussian_filter(rng.normal(size=(n, FINE, FINE)), (0, 1, 1))
    showers = np.exp(0.45 * showers / showers.std() - 0.1)     # small showers nobody can predict from the coarse field
    fine = intensity * terrain * showers * (rng.random((n, 1, 1)) < 0.7)   # 30% of days are dry
    coarse = fine.reshape(n, COARSE, 4, COARSE, 4).mean(axis=(2, 4))
    return fine, coarse

fine_tr, coarse_tr = rain_days(1500)
fine_te, coarse_te = rain_days(400)
fine_fu, coarse_fu = rain_days(400, scale=1.3)
bicubic = lambda c: np.stack([ndimage.zoom(x, 4, order=3, mode="nearest") for x in c]).clip(0, None)
bic_tr, bic_te, bic_fu = bicubic(coarse_tr), bicubic(coarse_te), bicubic(coarse_fu)
rmse = lambda a, b: np.sqrt(((a - b) ** 2).mean())
print(f"fine rain, training days: mean {fine_tr.mean():.2f} mm, 99th percentile {q99(fine_tr):.1f} mm, maximum {fine_tr.max():.0f} mm")
print(f"bicubic on test days: RMSE {rmse(bic_te, fine_te):.2f} mm")

5. A small super-resolution CNN with terrain as input¶

DeepSD (Vandal et al. 2017) stacks small super-resolution CNNs and gives each the high-resolution elevation as an extra input. Ours is one such stage: it takes the bicubic field, the elevation and their product, and predicts a correction to the bicubic field, trained with MSE. We score RMSE and the extremes: the 99th and 99.9th percentiles of all fine values, and the error of each pixel's own 99th percentile over the test days (the local heavy-rain level a district would plan for).

scale = bic_tr.std()
def features(b):
    b = b / scale
    e = np.broadcast_to(elev, b.shape)
    return torch.tensor(np.stack([b, e, b * e], axis=1), dtype=torch.float32)

class DownscaleCNN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, c=24):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(3, c, 5, padding=2), nn.ReLU(), nn.Conv2d(c, c, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Conv2d(c, 1, 3, padding=1))
    def forward(self, x):
        return x[:, 0] + self.net(x)[:, 0]                     # bicubic field plus a learned correction

t0 = time.time()
torch.manual_seed(0)
cnn = DownscaleCNN()
X, Y = features(bic_tr), torch.tensor(fine_tr / scale, dtype=torch.float32)
EPOCHS, BATCH = 10, 32
opt = torch.optim.Adam(cnn.parameters(), 2e-3)
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.OneCycleLR(opt, 3e-3, total_steps=EPOCHS * ((len(X) + BATCH - 1) // BATCH))
g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(0)
for epoch in range(EPOCHS):
    perm = torch.randperm(len(X), generator=g)
    for b in range(0, len(X), BATCH):
        idx = perm[b:b + BATCH]
        loss = ((cnn(X[idx]) - Y[idx]) ** 2).mean()
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step(); sched.step()
print(f"CNN: {sum(p.numel() for p in cnn.parameters()):,} weights, trained in {time.time() - t0:.0f} s")

def downscale(b):
    with torch.no_grad():
        return (cnn(features(b)).numpy() * scale).clip(0, None)

q999 = lambda x: np.quantile(x, 0.999)
local99 = lambda f: np.quantile(f, 0.99, axis=0)               # each pixel's own 99th percentile over the days
for label, fine, bic in [("test days", fine_te, bic_te), ("future days (30% heavier)", fine_fu, bic_fu)]:
    pred = {"bicubic": bic, "CNN": downscale(bic)}
    print(f"{label}: truth 99th pct {q99(fine):.1f} mm, 99.9th pct {q999(fine):.1f} mm")
    for name, f in pred.items():
        print(f"   {name:8s} RMSE {rmse(f, fine):.2f} mm | 99th pct {q99(f):5.1f} mm ({100 * (q99(f) / q99(fine) - 1):+.0f}%) | "
              f"99.9th pct {q999(f):5.1f} mm ({100 * (q999(f) / q999(fine) - 1):+.0f}%) | "
              f"local 99th pct: mean error {(local99(f) - local99(fine)).mean():+.1f} mm")
pred_te = downscale(bic_te)

day = int(np.argmax(coarse_te.mean(axis=(1, 2))))             # the wettest test day
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 5, figsize=(9, 2.3))
vmax = np.quantile(fine_te[day], 0.99)
for a, img, title in zip(ax, [elev, coarse_te[day], bic_te[day], pred_te[day], fine_te[day]],

6. Real data (NOT RUN here)¶

CMIP6 daily output is on Google Cloud in Zarr format, listed in the Pangeo catalogue. The cell takes one model's daily precipitation at the grid box nearest Dhaka for 1985–2014 (historical run) and 2071–2100 (a high-emission scenario, SSP5-8.5), and prints the model's own change in the 99th percentile. To correct it you need observed daily rain for the same place and years, for example a station record, and then qdm_multiplicative(obs, hist, fut) from section 3.

# NOT RUN: needs intake-esm, xarray, zarr and gcsfs (pip install intake-esm xarray zarr gcsfs) and internet access
import intake

catalogue = intake.open_esm_datastore("https://storage.googleapis.com/cmip6/pangeo-cmip6.json")
found = catalogue.search(source_id="MPI-ESM1-2-HR", member_id="r1i1p1f1", table_id="day", variable_id="pr",
                         experiment_id=["historical", "ssp585"])
dsets = found.to_dataset_dict(storage_options={"token": "anon"})

def grid_box(experiment, first_year, last_year):
    ds = next(v for k, v in dsets.items() if experiment in k)
    pr = ds["pr"].sel(lat=23.8, lon=90.4, method="nearest").sel(time=slice(first_year, last_year))
    return np.asarray(pr.values).squeeze() * 86400.0          # kg m-2 s-1 -> mm/day

hist = grid_box("historical", "1985", "2014")
fut = grid_box("ssp585", "2071", "2100")

What to notice¶

(Written from the run above; your numbers will match if you keep the seeds.)

  1. Quantile mapping fits the past perfectly, by construction. Applied to the historical run, QM reproduced the observed temperature (mean 33.01, standard deviation 2.50) and the observed rain: 0.435 rain days and a 99th percentile of 44.3 mm, removing the model's drizzle (0.758 rain days). A perfect fit on the calibration period says nothing about the future.
  2. The methods disagree about the future. The model warmed by +2.00 °C on average and +3.10 °C at the 99th percentile. The delta method kept the mean change but gave hot days only +2.00. QM inflated both changes, to +2.77 and +4.07, because stretching the model's narrow spread (1.8) to the observed one (2.5) also stretched its change. QDM kept both: +2.00 and +3.11.
  3. Simple quantile mapping caps new records. 152 future model days were hotter than any historical model day, and QM mapped every one of them to 42.86 °C, the hottest observed day. QM's maximum stayed at 42.86; QDM's reached 45.51.
  4. For rain, only QDM kept the change in heavy rain. The model's mean rose 3.9% and its 99th percentile 9.2%. The delta method raised everything by 3.9% and missed the stronger rise in heavy rain; QM gave +10.9%; QDM gave +9.1%. QDM did not keep the change in the mean (+6.0% against +3.9%), because it keeps the change at each quantile and the observed distribution weights the quantiles differently.
  5. The CNN beat bicubic on RMSE, much less on extremes. On test days RMSE fell from 4.27 to 3.71 mm (13%), because the CNN learned that hills get more rain. Both still underestimated heavy rain: the 99th percentile by 8% (bicubic) and 7% (CNN), the 99.9th by 12% and 8%, and each pixel's own 99th percentile by 3.7 and 3.3 mm on average. The small showers cannot be predicted from the coarse field, and a model trained with MSE predicts their average, as in session 7.
  6. On the heavier future days the picture stayed the same. The CNN kept its RMSE lead (4.51 against 5.06 mm) and underestimated the 99th percentile by 6% (bicubic 8%); its local 99th-percentile error (−4.9 mm) was no better than bicubic's (−4.8 mm). This future was only 30% heavier and shaped like the past; a real future can change in ways no training data showed.
