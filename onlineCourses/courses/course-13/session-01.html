1. From problems to machine learning tasks¶

Most problems in this course fall into four task types: detect (classify a photo or a reading), map (label every pixel of a satellite image), forecast (predict a number in the future) and decide (choose an action when the future is uncertain). For each problem, write down three things before touching a model: what the input data are, where the labels come from, and who will use the prediction for what. The last question tells you how to split the data for validation.

2. A synthetic district panel¶

We need data where we know the truth, so we build it. The panel has 64 "districts" on an 8 × 8 grid (Bangladesh also has 64 districts, but this grid is not its map) and 20 years, 2005–2024: 1,280 district-year rows. The 64 districts are grouped into 8 regions of 4 × 2 districts, like administrative divisions.

Yield (tonnes per hectare) is built from:

  • district effects: soil quality and irrigation share (both observed, both smooth in space), plus a hidden district effect we do not give the model (farm management, markets);
  • weather that is correlated in space: a seasonal rainfall index (too little hurts rainfed districts, too much floods everyone) and a count of heat days (each day above 3 costs yield);
  • year effects: a technology trend of +0.04 t/ha per year, and a hidden year shock (pests, prices) shared by nearby districts;
  • survey noise of 0.12 t/ha.

The model sees year, grid row and column, soil, irrigation, rain and heat days. It never sees the hidden district effect or the year shock, but it can partly recognize a district from its coordinates and a year from its year number. That is exactly what makes random cross-validation look good.

The real data this stands in for: district yields from the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics' Yearbook of Agricultural Statistics, with seasonal weather from a reanalysis such as ERA5-Land (section 7).

rng = np.random.default_rng(15)
G, YEARS = 8, np.arange(2005, 2025)
nD, nY = G * G, len(YEARS)
row, col = np.divmod(np.arange(nD), G)
region = (row // 4) * 4 + (col // 2)                  # 8 regions of 4 x 2 districts

def smooth_field(sigma=1.5):
    # a random map that is smooth in space (neighbouring districts are similar), mean 0, sd 1
    f = gaussian_filter(rng.normal(size=(G, G)), sigma, mode="reflect").ravel()
    return (f - f.mean()) / f.std()

soil = smooth_field()
irrig = np.clip(0.55 + 0.2 * smooth_field(), 0.05, 0.95)
u_dist = 0.35 * smooth_field(1.2)                                  # hidden district effect
rain_clim = 1.0 + 0.5 * (col / (G - 1) - 0.5) + 0.1 * smooth_field()  # wetter in the east
heat_clim = 4.0 - 4.0 * (col / (G - 1) - 0.5)                      # hotter in the west

records = []
for t, yr in enumerate(YEARS):
    rain = rain_clim * np.exp(0.25 * rng.normal() + 0.15 * smooth_field(2.0))     # national + local anomaly
    heat = np.clip(heat_clim + 2.5 * rng.normal() + 1.5 * smooth_field(2.0), 0, None)
    shock = 0.30 * rng.normal() + 0.20 * smooth_field(2.0)                         # hidden year shock
    g_rain = -1.5 * np.maximum(0, 0.85 - rain) * (1 - irrig) - 0.9 * np.maximum(0, rain - 1.35)
    h_heat = -0.10 * np.maximum(0, heat - 3.0)
    y = (3.2 + 0.04 * t + 0.25 * soil + 1.2 * irrig + u_dist + g_rain + h_heat + shock
         + rng.normal(0, 0.12, nD))
    for d in range(nD):
        records.append((d, yr, row[d], col[d], region[d], soil[d], irrig[d], rain[d], heat[d], y[d]))
df = pd.DataFrame(records, columns=["district", "year", "row", "col", "region", "soil", "irrigation",
                                    "rain", "heat_days", "yield"])

X, y = df[FEATS].to_numpy(), df["yield"].to_numpy()
print(f"panel: {len(df)} rows = {nD} districts x {nY} years; features {FEATS}")
print(df[["soil", "irrigation", "rain", "heat_days", "yield"]].describe().loc[["mean", "std", "min", "max"]].round(2))

3. Random K-fold cross-validation¶

Random 5-fold cross-validation shuffles the 1,280 rows and holds out a fifth at a time. We use a gradient-boosted tree model (HistGradientBoostingRegressor, the scikit-learn version of LightGBM-style boosting; Course 6 session 5 covers boosted trees) and report the root mean squared error (RMSE) and $R^2$ on the held-out rows. The baseline "predict the training mean" shows how much the model adds.

def cv_predict(splitter, groups=None, X=X, y=y):
    # out-of-fold predictions: every row is predicted by a model that did not train on it
    pred, base = np.zeros_like(y), np.zeros_like(y)
    for tr, te in splitter.split(X, y, groups):
        m = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(max_iter=200, learning_rate=0.08, random_state=0).fit(X[tr], y[tr])
        pred[te], base[te] = m.predict(X[te]), y[tr].mean()
    return pred, base

def scores(pred, base, y=y):
    rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((pred - y) ** 2))
    return rmse, 1 - np.mean((pred - y) ** 2) / np.var(y), np.sqrt(np.mean((base - y) ** 2))

results, oof = {}, {}
oof["random 5-fold"], base = cv_predict(KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0))
results["random 5-fold"] = scores(oof["random 5-fold"], base)
rmse, r2, b = results["random 5-fold"]
print(f"random 5-fold: RMSE {rmse:.3f} t/ha, R^2 {r2:.3f}  (predict-the-mean baseline RMSE {b:.3f})")

random 5-fold: RMSE 0.209 t/ha, R^2 0.909  (predict-the-mean baseline RMSE 0.694)

4. Leave-one-year-out and leave-region-out¶

Now copy how the model will be used (Course 6 session 3: "copy the test split").

  • Leave-one-year-out (20 folds): train on 19 years, test on all 64 districts in the held-out year. This is the question "how good will next year's estimate be?" The model cannot learn that year's hidden shock from other districts in the same year.
  • Leave-region-out (8 folds): train on 7 regions, test on all years of the held-out region. This is "how good is the model in a region we have no data from?" The model cannot recognize those districts.
  • New region and new years (40 folds): hold out one region in one 4-year block, and drop from training every row of that region and every row of those years. This is the hardest and most honest test for "a new district, next year".
yrs, reg = df["year"].to_numpy(), df["region"].to_numpy()
oof["leave-one-year-out"], base = cv_predict(LeaveOneGroupOut(), yrs)
results["leave-one-year-out"] = scores(oof["leave-one-year-out"], base)
oof["leave-region-out"], base = cv_predict(LeaveOneGroupOut(), reg)
results["leave-region-out"] = scores(oof["leave-region-out"], base)

pred_b, base_b, yblock = np.zeros_like(y), np.zeros_like(y), (yrs - 2005) // 4
for r_ in range(8):
    for b in range(5):
        te = (reg == r_) & (yblock == b)
        tr = (reg != r_) & (yblock != b)               # neither the same region nor the same years
        m = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(max_iter=200, learning_rate=0.08, random_state=0).fit(X[tr], y[tr])
        pred_b[te], base_b[te] = m.predict(X[te]), y[tr].mean()
oof["new region and new years"] = pred_b
results["new region and new years"] = scores(pred_b, base_b)

print(f"{'validation':26s} {'RMSE':>6s} {'R^2':>6s}  {'mean-baseline RMSE':>18s}")
for k, (rmse, r2, b) in results.items():
    print(f"{k:26s} {rmse:6.3f} {r2:6.3f}  {b:18.3f}")
e_year = pd.Series((oof["leave-one-year-out"] - y) ** 2).groupby(yrs).mean() ** 0.5
print(f"leave-one-year-out RMSE in the first year (2005) {e_year[2005]:.3f}, last year (2024) {e_year[2024]:.3f}, "
      f"median year {e_year.median():.3f}")
r0 = results["random 5-fold"][0]
print(f"honest RMSE / random-CV RMSE: year {results['leave-one-year-out'][0] / r0:.2f}x, "
      f"region {results['leave-region-out'][0] / r0:.2f}x, both {results['new region and new years'][0] / r0:.2f}x")

6. More rows from the same places and years¶

Suppose a survey visits more fields in the same districts and years: each district-year now has $k$ fields, whose yields differ from the district-year value by field noise (standard deviation 0.3 t/ha), with the same weather features. The data set grows from 1,280 to $1,280k$ rows. Does the model become more general? Random cross-validation says yes. Leave-region-out says no: the new rows carry no new information about new places.

rng_f = np.random.default_rng(16)
print(f"{'fields per district-year':>24s} {'rows':>6s} {'random 5-fold RMSE':>19s} {'leave-region-out RMSE':>22s}")
for k in [1, 4]:
    dfk = df.loc[df.index.repeat(k)].reset_index(drop=True)
    yk = dfk["yield"].to_numpy() + rng_f.normal(0, 0.30, len(dfk))          # field yields
    Xk = dfk[FEATS].to_numpy()
    p_r, _ = cv_predict(KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0), None, Xk, yk)
    p_g, _ = cv_predict(LeaveOneGroupOut(), dfk["region"].to_numpy(), Xk, yk)
    print(f"{k:24d} {len(dfk):6d} {np.sqrt(np.mean((p_r - yk) ** 2)):19.3f} {np.sqrt(np.mean((p_g - yk) ** 2)):22.3f}")

7. The same experiment on real data (NOT RUN here)¶

To repeat this on real data you need two tables: district yields by year (typed in from the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics' Yearbook of Agricultural Statistics) and weather for each district and year. The cell below downloads ERA5-Land monthly means for the Boro growing months from the Copernicus Climate Data Store and takes the grid cell nearest each district centre. ds = xr.open_dataset("era5land_boro_months.nc")

What to notice¶

(Written from the run above; your numbers will match if you keep the seeds.)

  1. Same model, same data, four answers. Random 5-fold cross-validation gave an RMSE of 0.209 t/ha ($R^2$ 0.909). Leave-one-year-out gave 0.418 ($R^2$ 0.634), leave-region-out 0.470 ($R^2$ 0.538), and a new region in new years 0.570 ($R^2$ 0.321): 2.00, 2.25 and 2.73 times the random-CV error. Predicting the training mean gives 0.69 to 0.77, so the model still helps, but much less than random cross-validation claims.
  2. Why random cross-validation is optimistic. In random folds, the other 19 years of a test district and the other 63 districts of a test year are in the training set. The model recognizes districts by their grid coordinates and years by their year number, and so learns the hidden district effects and year shocks that it was never given.
  3. Against expectation, the trend was not the main problem in leave-one-year-out. I expected the first and last years to be worst, because a tree model cannot extend a trend beyond the years it has seen. They were not: 2005 had an RMSE of 0.274 and 2024 had 0.360, against 0.372 for the median year. Here the hidden year shocks mattered more than the trend; session 5 builds a case where extrapolation dominates.
  4. The error map shows where the model extrapolates. Under random cross-validation the district mean errors ranged from 0.103 to 0.315 t/ha, with no warning sign anywhere. Under leave-region-out they ranged from 0.133 to 1.103: region R6 (0.65), R7 (0.44) and R3 (0.42, which holds the worst district in the top-right corner) were much worse than R1 (0.26). These are the regions whose soil, irrigation or hidden effects are least like the rest.
  5. More rows from the same places and years did not make the model more general. With four fields per district-year (5,120 rows instead of 1,280), the random-CV RMSE fell from 0.402 to 0.335, close to the field-noise floor of 0.300, because sibling fields of the same district-year sit in the training folds. Leave-region-out improved only from 0.601 to 0.567, probably because averaging four noisy fields gives cleaner training labels; the extra rows told the model almost nothing about new places.
