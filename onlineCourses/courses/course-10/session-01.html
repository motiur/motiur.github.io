1. Angles: the average of 350° and 10°¶

An angle lives on a circle, not on a line: 350° and 10° are only 20° apart, but as numbers they are 340 apart. Work out on paper first:

  • the arithmetic mean of 350° and 10°;
  • the circular mean: turn each angle $\theta_i$ into the point $(\cos\theta_i, \sin\theta_i)$ on the unit circle, average the points, and read off the angle of the average with $\operatorname{atan2}(\bar y, \bar x)$.

The length $\bar R$ of the averaged point (the mean resultant length) is 1 when all angles agree and near 0 when they are spread around the circle (Mardia & Jupp 2000). Then the cell does the same for a week of hourly wind directions. The data are synthetic (drawn from a von Mises distribution, the circular analogue of the normal distribution, centred on north); real data would be hourly records from a weather station.

import time
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import linalg, stats
from scipy.spatial.transform import Rotation, Slerp

t_start = time.time()
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def circular_mean_deg(angles_deg):
    # average the points (cos, sin) on the unit circle; return (mean angle in [0, 360), mean resultant length)
    z = np.exp(1j * np.radians(angles_deg)).mean()
    return round(float(np.degrees(np.angle(z))), 9) % 360, abs(z)

pair = np.array([350.0, 10.0])

2. Places on Earth: great-circle distance against straight-line distance¶

A place on Earth is a point on a sphere of radius $R = 6{,}371$ km (the mean radius). With latitude $\varphi$ and longitude $\lambda$ in radians, the shortest path along the surface (the great-circle distance) is given by the haversine formula $$d = 2R\arcsin\sqrt{\sin^2\tfrac{\Delta\varphi}{2} + \cos\varphi_1\cos\varphi_2\sin^2\tfrac{\Delta\lambda}{2}}.$$ The straight line through the Earth (the chord) has length $c = 2R\sin\big(d/(2R)\big)$, which is always shorter. A third, common mistake is to treat (latitude, longitude) as a point in the plane and use the Euclidean distance, with $2\pi R/360 \approx 111.2$ km per degree. The coordinates are approximate city centres (Wikipedia), rounded to 0.01°; different sources differ by a few kilometres.

R_EARTH = 6371.0
cities = {"Dhaka": (23.76, 90.39), "Chattogram": (22.34, 91.83), "Sylhet": (24.90, 91.87),
          "London": (51.51, -0.13), "Tokyo": (35.69, 139.69)}                   # (latitude N, longitude E), degrees

def unit_vector(lat, lon):
    la, lo = np.radians(lat), np.radians(lon)
    return np.array([np.cos(la) * np.cos(lo), np.cos(la) * np.sin(lo), np.sin(la)])

def haversine_km(p, q):
    (la1, lo1), (la2, lo2) = np.radians(p), np.radians(q)
    h = np.sin((la2 - la1) / 2) ** 2 + np.cos(la1) * np.cos(la2) * np.sin((lo2 - lo1) / 2) ** 2
    return 2 * R_EARTH * np.arcsin(np.sqrt(h))

3. Covariance matrices: the swelling effect¶

A covariance matrix is symmetric positive definite (SPD): $C = C^\top$ and all eigenvalues are positive. Its determinant (the product of the eigenvalues) measures the volume of its ellipse $\{x : x^\top C^{-1} x \le 1\}$, whose area is $\pi\sqrt{\det C}$ in 2-D.

By hand: $A = \operatorname{diag}(9, 1)$ and $B = \operatorname{diag}(1, 9)$ both have determinant 9. What is the determinant of their entrywise (Euclidean) average? Now take matrix logarithms (for a diagonal matrix, the log of each diagonal entry), average them and exponentiate: this is the log-Euclidean mean (Arsigny et al. 2007). What is its determinant?

Because $\det e^{M} = e^{\operatorname{tr} M}$, the log-Euclidean mean always has determinant equal to the geometric mean of the determinants. The Euclidean mean's determinant is never smaller (Minkowski's determinant inequality): it "swells". We check both claims, also on matrices that do not commute, and on 50 random 2×2 matrices with determinant 1.

def spd_log(C):                                  # matrix logarithm of an SPD matrix through its eigendecomposition
    w, V = np.linalg.eigh(C); return (V * np.log(w)) @ V.T

def spd_exp(S):                                  # matrix exponential of a symmetric matrix
    w, V = np.linalg.eigh(S); return (V * np.exp(w)) @ V.T

def log_euclidean_mean(Cs):
    return spd_exp(np.mean([spd_log(C) for C in Cs], axis=0))

def rot2(deg):
    t = np.radians(deg); return np.array([[np.cos(t), -np.sin(t)], [np.sin(t), np.cos(t)]])

A, B = np.diag([9.0, 1.0]), np.diag([1.0, 9.0])
E, L = (A + B) / 2, log_euclidean_mean([A, B])
print(f"diag(9, 1) and diag(1, 9): Euclidean mean diag({E[0, 0]:.3f}, {E[1, 1]:.3f}), det {np.linalg.det(E):.3f}; "
      f"log-Euclidean mean diag({L[0, 0]:.3f}, {L[1, 1]:.3f}), det {np.linalg.det(L):.3f}")

A2 = np.diag([4.0, 0.25]); B2 = rot2(60) @ A2 @ rot2(60).T                  # same shape, turned by 60 degrees
E2, L2 = (A2 + B2) / 2, log_euclidean_mean([A2, B2])
print(f"diag(4, 1/4) and the same ellipse turned by 60 degrees (both det 1): Euclidean mean det {np.linalg.det(E2):.4f} "
      f"(eigenvalues {np.linalg.eigvalsh(E2).round(4)}), log-Euclidean mean det {np.linalg.det(L2):.4f} (eigenvalues {np.linalg.eigvalsh(L2).round(4)})")
print(f"check against scipy.linalg.logm/expm: largest difference {np.abs(linalg.expm((linalg.logm(A2) + linalg.logm(B2)).real / 2) - L2).max():.1e}")

4. Rotations: averaging rotation matrices leaves the rotations¶

A rotation matrix satisfies $R^\top R = I$ and $\det R = +1$; these matrices form the group SO(3). By hand: the midpoint of the identity $I$ and the rotation by 90° about the z-axis is $$\tfrac12\left(I + R_z(90°)\right) = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & -0.5 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$ What are its determinant and the lengths of its columns? What is the midpoint of $I$ and $R_z(180°)$?

The fix is to interpolate along the rotations themselves. A rotation by angle $\alpha$ about the unit axis $n$ is the unit quaternion $q = (\sin\frac\alpha2\, n, \cos\frac\alpha2)$ (SciPy puts the scalar last), and slerp moves along the great circle between two unit quaternions (Shoemake 1985): $$\operatorname{slerp}(q_0, q_1; t) = \frac{\sin((1-t)\Omega)}{\sin\Omega} q_0 + \frac{\sin(t\Omega)}{\sin\Omega} q_1, \qquad \cos\Omega = q_0\cdot q_1.$$ Since $q$ and $-q$ are the same rotation, flip $q_1$ when $q_0 \cdot q_1 < 0$ to take the short way. A third option, interpolating linearly and then projecting back to the nearest rotation with an SVD, stays on SO(3) but does not turn at a constant speed.

R0 = np.eye(3)
for deg in [90, 180]:
    M = (R0 + Rotation.from_euler("z", deg, degrees=True).as_matrix()) / 2
    print(f"midpoint of I and R_z({deg}): det {np.linalg.det(M):.3f}, column lengths {np.linalg.norm(M, axis=0).round(3)}, "
          f"orthogonality error ||M^T M - I|| = {np.linalg.norm(M.T @ M - np.eye(3)):.3f}")

def slerp(q0, q1, t):
    d = q0 @ q1
    if d < 0: q1, d = -q1, -d                             # q and -q are the same rotation: take the short way
    om = np.arccos(np.clip(d, -1.0, 1.0))
    return (np.sin((1 - t) * om) * q0 + np.sin(t * om) * q1) / np.sin(om)

def nearest_rotation(M):                                  # project a 3x3 matrix onto SO(3) with an SVD
    U, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(M)
    D = np.diag([1.0, 1.0, np.sign(np.linalg.det(U @ Vt))])
    return U @ D @ Vt

R1 = Rotation.from_euler("z", 90, degrees=True)
q0, q1 = Rotation.identity().as_quat(), R1.as_quat()
print(f"quaternion of R_z(90), scalar last: {q1.round(4)}")
scipy_slerp = Slerp([0, 1], Rotation.concatenate([Rotation.identity(), R1]))

    Rs = Rotation.from_quat(slerp(q0, q1, t))

    ang_slerp.append(np.degrees(Rs.magnitude())); ang_proj.append(np.degrees(Rotation.from_matrix(nearest_rotation(M)).magnitude()))

      f"linear-then-project {ang_proj[10]:.2f} degrees (atan(1/3) = {np.degrees(np.arctan(1 / 3)):.2f})")

  1. Angles wrap around. The arithmetic mean of 350° and 10° is 180°, the opposite direction; the circular mean is 0°, with mean resultant length 0.9848 (= cos 10°). For the 168 synthetic wind directions, 49% of which lie just west of north (above 180°), the arithmetic mean was 178.2°, almost due south, while the circular mean was 0.8° (scipy.stats.circmean agrees) with $\bar R$ = 0.860.
  2. The sphere is flat up close and curved far away. Inside Bangladesh the chord is only 8 to 24 m shorter than the great-circle distance (196.3 to 284.7 km). For Dhaka–London (7,998.2 km) the chord is 514.973 km shorter, and for London–Tokyo 871.578 km. Treating (latitude, longitude) as a flat plane was 4.1% to 5.8% too long for Dhaka's neighbours, exact for Chattogram–Sylhet (they lie almost on the same meridian, so only the shrinking longitude degrees cause the error), 31.6% too long for Dhaka–London and 63.7% for London–Tokyo.
  3. Precision matters near 1. For a point 1 km north of Dhaka, $\arccos(u \cdot v)$ in float32 returned 0.0000 km, because $u \cdot v$ rounded to exactly 1; the haversine formula gave 1.0000 km. The same problem returns with $\operatorname{arcosh}$ in hyperbolic space (sessions 4 and 6).
  4. Euclidean means of SPD matrices swell. diag(9, 1) and diag(1, 9) both have determinant 9; their Euclidean mean diag(5, 5) has determinant 25 and their log-Euclidean mean diag(3, 3) has determinant 9. For two ellipses of determinant 1 turned 60° apart, the Euclidean mean had determinant 3.6367 and the log-Euclidean mean 1.0000; for 50 random matrices of determinant 1, 1.671 against 1.000. Our eigendecomposition formulas matched scipy.linalg.logm/expm to 1.1e-16.
  5. Averaging rotation matrices leaves SO(3). The midpoint of $I$ and $R_z(90°)$ has determinant 0.500 and columns of length 0.707; the midpoint of $I$ and $R_z(180°)$ is diag(0, 0, 1), with determinant 0, which flattens every vector onto the z-axis. Slerp stayed on SO(3) and matched SciPy's Slerp to 4.4e-16. Projecting the straight-line interpolation back with an SVD also gives rotations, but not at a constant speed: 18.43° instead of 22.50° at $t = 0.25$.
  6. Every result here matched the hand calculation. The common lesson: storing curved data as numbers is fine, but averages, distances and interpolation must use operations of the space itself. Sessions 2 and 3 build those operations (tangent spaces, geodesics, exponential and logarithm maps, Fréchet means) on the sphere.
