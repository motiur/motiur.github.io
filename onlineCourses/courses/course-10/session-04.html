1. Circles in three geometries¶

Draw all points at distance $r$ from a centre and measure the length of the resulting circle:

  • on the unit sphere (curvature $K = 1$) it is a latitude circle: $C(r) = 2\pi\sin r$;
  • in the plane ($K = 0$): $C(r) = 2\pi r$;
  • in the hyperbolic plane ($K = -1$): $C(r) = 2\pi\sinh r$, which grows like $\pi e^r$.

Curvature is visible in how fast circles grow. For small $r$, $C(r) \approx 2\pi r - \frac{\pi}{3}K r^3$, so $K \approx \frac{3(2\pi r - C(r))}{\pi r^3}$ (the Bertrand–Diguet–Puiseux formula). We check $2\pi\sinh r$ directly in the Poincaré disk, the model of the hyperbolic plane used in this course: the unit disk with distance $$d(x, y) = \operatorname{arcosh}\Big(1 + \frac{2\|x - y\|^2}{(1 - \|x\|^2)(1 - \|y\|^2)}\Big).$$ The hyperbolic circle of radius $r$ around the origin is the Euclidean circle of radius $\tanh(r/2)$; we add up the hyperbolic lengths of 20,000 short chords around it. A tree with branching 3 has $3^k$ nodes at depth $k$: exponential growth, like hyperbolic circles.

2. Triangles: angle sums in three geometries¶

For a geodesic triangle, Gauss–Bonnet says (angle sum) $- \pi = \iint K\,dA$: more than $\pi$ on the sphere, exactly $\pi$ in the plane, less than $\pi$ in the hyperbolic plane (do Carmo 1976). Take an equilateral triangle with sides $a = 1$. The laws of cosines give its angle $\alpha$:

  • sphere: $\cos a = \cos^2 a + \sin^2 a\cos\alpha$, so $\cos\alpha = \frac{\cos a}{1 + \cos a}$;
  • hyperbolic plane: $\cosh a = \cosh^2 a - \sinh^2 a\cos\alpha$, so $\cos\alpha = \frac{\cosh a}{1 + \cosh a}$.

Compute both on a calculator first. We then check the areas independently. On the sphere: build the triangle from three unit vectors and use the area formula from session 3. In the Poincaré disk: put one corner at the origin and two at hyperbolic distance 1 with angle $\alpha$ between them (the disk is conformal, so angles at the origin are ordinary angles), check that the third side is 1, and integrate the area element. To do the integral we map the triangle to the Klein model $k = 2p/(1 + \|p\|^2)$, where hyperbolic geodesics are straight lines and the area element is $(1 - \|k\|^2)^{-3/2}\,dk$.

a = 1.0

3. Distances in the Poincaré disk, and the same space in another model¶

By hand: the distance from the origin to $x$ simplifies to $d(0, x) = 2\operatorname{artanh}\|x\| = \ln\frac{1 + \|x\|}{1 - \|x\|}$. What is $d(0, (0.5, 0))$? It equals $\ln 3$.

Near the boundary, small Euclidean gaps are long: Course 7 session 8 asked whether two points at radius 0.99 that look close are close. Here we measure it. Then two facts that show the disk is only a picture of hyperbolic space:

  • the Möbius map $T_a(z) = \frac{z - a}{1 - \bar a z}$ (complex numbers) moves the point $a$ to the centre and keeps every distance: the centre is not a special place;
  • the upper half-plane $\{w : \operatorname{Im} w > 0\}$ with $d(w_1, w_2) = \operatorname{arcosh}\big(1 + \frac{|w_1 - w_2|^2}{2\operatorname{Im}w_1\operatorname{Im}w_2}\big)$ is another model of the same space; the map $w = i\frac{1 + z}{1 - z}$ carries the disk onto it and keeps every distance. The hyperboloid (Lorentz) model comes in session 6.
print(f"d(0, 0.5) = {disk_dist(0j, 0.5 + 0j):.10f}, ln 3 = {np.log(3):.10f}, 2 artanh(0.5) = {2 * np.arctanh(0.5):.10f}")
x0 = 0.99 + 0j; y0 = 0.99 * np.exp(1j * 0.01 / 0.99)                  # two points at radius 0.99, a Euclidean gap of 0.01
print(f"Euclidean gap {abs(x0 - y0):.4f} at radius 0.99: hyperbolic distance {disk_dist(x0, y0):.4f}; "
      f"the same gap at the centre: {disk_dist(0j, 0.01 + 0j):.4f}")

4. A tree embedded by construction (Sarkar 2011)¶

Sarkar's construction places a tree in the hyperbolic plane with every edge of hyperbolic length $\tau$ (the scaling factor):

  1. put the root at the origin and its children evenly around the circle of hyperbolic radius $\tau$ (Euclidean radius $\tanh(\tau/2)$);
  2. for every other node $v$ with parent $p$: move $v$ to the origin with the Möbius map $T_v$; the parent lands in some direction $\theta_p$; put the children of $v$ at hyperbolic distance $\tau$ in the directions $\theta_p + 2\pi k/\deg(v)$, $k = 1, \dots, \deg(v) - 1$, so the parent's direction is left free; then map back with $T_v^{-1}(w) = \frac{w + v}{1 + \bar v w}$.

Because $T_v$ keeps distances, every edge has length exactly $\tau$, and each node's edges leave it at equal angles (90° for our nodes of degree 4). Our tree is networkx.balanced_tree(3, 5): branching 3, depth 5, 364 nodes, 243 leaves. The Euclidean baseline is a radial layout: depth $k$ on the circle of radius $k$, each subtree in an angular wedge proportional to its number of leaves. Edges in the disk are drawn as geodesics (arcs of circles that meet the boundary at right angles): $t \mapsto T_v^{-1}(t\,T_v(c))$.

G = nx.balanced_tree(3, 5)
n = G.number_of_nodes()
parent = {child: par for par, child in nx.bfs_edges(G, 0)}
depth = nx.shortest_path_length(G, 0)
children = {v: [c for c in G.neighbors(v) if parent.get(v) != c] for v in G}
print(f"tree: {n} nodes, {sum(G.degree(v) == 1 for v in G)} leaves, depth {max(depth.values())}")

def sarkar_embedding(tau):
    z = np.zeros(n, dtype=complex)
    r = np.tanh(tau / 2)
    for k, c in enumerate(children[0]):
        z[c] = r * np.exp(2j * np.pi * k / G.degree(0))
    for v in nx.bfs_tree(G, 0):
        if v == 0: continue
        theta_p = np.angle(T(z[v], z[parent[v]]))                         # direction of the parent after moving v to 0
        for k, c in enumerate(children[v], start=1):
            wk = r * np.exp(1j * (theta_p + 2 * np.pi * k / G.degree(v)))
            z[c] = (wk + z[v]) / (1 + np.conj(z[v]) * wk)                 # back with T_v^{-1}
    return z

def radial_layout():
    leaves_below = {}
    for v in reversed(list(nx.bfs_tree(G, 0))):
        leaves_below[v] = 1 if not children[v] else sum(leaves_below[c] for c in children[v])
    pos, wedge = np.zeros((n, 2)), {0: (0.0, 2 * np.pi)}
    for v in nx.bfs_tree(G, 0):
        lo, hi = wedge[v]; mid, width = (lo + hi) / 2, hi - lo
        pos[v] = depth[v] * np.array([np.cos(mid), np.sin(mid)])
        for c in children[v]:                                              # split the wedge by numbers of leaves
            span = width * leaves_below[c] / leaves_below[v]; wedge[c] = (lo, lo + span); lo += span
    return pos

z2 = sarkar_embedding(2.0)
edge_len = np.array([disk_dist(z2[u_], z2[v_]) for u_, v_ in G.edges()])
print(f"tau = 2: edge lengths between {edge_len.min():.10f} and {edge_len.max():.10f}; largest |z| = {np.abs(z2).max():.6f}")
pos = radial_layout()

5. Distortion as the edge length grows¶

Compare the embedded distance $d_E(u, v)$ with the tree distance $d_G(u, v)$ (number of edges, from networkx shortest paths) over all 66,066 pairs of nodes. With the ratio $\rho_{uv} = d_E(u,v)/d_G(u,v)$:

  • worst-case distortion $= \max\rho / \min\rho$ (1 is perfect, and it does not depend on the scale);
  • average distortion $= \operatorname{mean}_{uv}\,|s\,\rho_{uv} - 1|$ (Sala et al. 2018), where the scale $s$ is chosen for each embedding to make it as small as possible (a weighted median), so that neither embedding is penalized for its units.

By hand: in the radial layout, two sibling leaves sit on the circle of radius 5, about $2\pi \cdot 5/243 \approx 0.13$ apart, while their tree distance is 2. What does this do to the worst-case distortion?

D_tree = np.zeros((n, n))
for s_, lengths in nx.all_pairs_shortest_path_length(G):
    for t_, l in lengths.items(): D_tree[s_, t_] = l
iu = np.triu_indices(n, 1)
dG = D_tree[iu]

def disk_pair_dists(z, dtype=np.float64):
    x, y = z.real.astype(dtype), z.imag.astype(dtype)
    xi, yi, xj, yj = x[iu[0]], y[iu[0]], x[iu[1]], y[iu[1]]
    one, two = dtype(1), dtype(2)
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore", over="ignore"):      # float32 failures are reported, not warned about
        return np.arccosh(one + two * ((xi - xj) ** 2 + (yi - yj) ** 2) / ((one - xi ** 2 - yi ** 2) * (one - xj ** 2 - yj ** 2)))

def distortion(dE):
    rho = dE / dG
    if not np.all(np.isfinite(rho)) or rho.min() <= 0:
        return np.nan, np.nan
    xs, ws = 1 / rho, rho
    order = np.argsort(xs); cw = np.cumsum(ws[order])
    s = xs[order][np.searchsorted(cw, cw[-1] / 2)]                    # weighted median: the best scale
    return np.mean(np.abs(s * rho - 1)), rho.max() / rho.min()

pos = radial_layout()
radial_avg, radial_wc = distortion(np.linalg.norm(pos[iu[0]] - pos[iu[1]], axis=1))

6. The price: precision near the boundary¶

Longer edges push the leaves out: a leaf at depth 5 is at hyperbolic distance $5\tau$ from the root, at Euclidean radius $\tanh(5\tau/2)$, so $1 - \|z\| \approx 2e^{-5\tau}$. A floating-point number just below 1 is at best $1 - 2^{-24}$ in float32 and $1 - 2^{-53}$ in float64, so the largest distance from the origin that can be stored is $\ln\frac{1 + r}{1 - r}$ at that $r$. We store the embeddings in float32, recompute all distances in float32, and compare with float64.

What to notice¶

(Written from the run above; your numbers will match if you keep the seeds.)

  1. Circles measure curvature. From the circumference at $r = 0.01$, the estimated curvature was +0.999995 (sphere), 0 (plane) and −1.000005 (hyperbolic). In the Poincaré disk, 20,000 chords around the circle of radius 2 added up to 22.788235 against $2\pi\sinh 2 = 22.788236$; at radius 5 the chord sum (466.221964) fell slightly short of 466.232522, because chords are shorter than arcs and the circle is long.
  2. Triangles measure curvature too. With sides 1, the angle sums were 208.3955° (sphere), 180° (plane) and 157.9297° (hyperbolic). The independently computed areas matched Gauss–Bonnet: 0.495595 for the spherical excess and 0.385199 for the hyperbolic deficit, the latter from an integral in the Klein model.
  3. The disk is only a picture. $d(0, 0.5) = \ln 3 = 1.0986122887$. A Euclidean gap of 0.01 is a hyperbolic distance of 0.9669 at radius 0.99 but 0.0200 at the centre. A Möbius map moved $0.3 + 0.5i$ to the centre and changed no distance by more than 2.3e-14, and the upper half-plane model gave the same distances to 4.4e-15: the same space, drawn differently.
  4. Sarkar's construction needs long edges, and then it wins. Against expectation, short edges did badly: at $\tau$ = 0.5 the average distortion (0.3085) was worse than the Euclidean radial layout's (0.2630), and the worst case was 657.86 against 107.62, because branches overlapped (the closest two nodes were 0.0122 $\tau$ apart). From $\tau$ = 2 on, the closest pair was an edge and the distortion fell steadily: worst case 1.8268 at $\tau$ = 2, 1.2986 at 3 and 1.1133 at 6; average 0.0609, 0.0341 and 0.0156. The radial layout cannot do this: adjacent leaves on either side of the root's wedges are about 0.13 apart but 10 edges apart in the tree.
  5. Low distortion costs precision. Leaves sit at distance $5\tau$ from the root. float32 can store points only up to distance 17.33 from the origin (float64: 37.43), so at $\tau$ = 4 (leaves at 20) 69 points rounded onto the boundary and 68.0% of the distances were off by more than 1%; at $\tau$ = 3 float32 still worked (worst case 1.3107 against 1.2986 in float64). Even float64 lost accuracy before its limit: at $\tau$ = 6 the edge lengths, exactly 6 by construction, came out between 5.999241 and 6.000427.
  6. The trade-off is real: lower distortion needs longer edges, longer edges need more bits. Session 6 studies these numerical limits in both the Poincaré and the Lorentz models; session 7 trains embeddings instead of constructing them.
