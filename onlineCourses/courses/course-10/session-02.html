1. By hand: tangent vectors of the sphere¶

The unit sphere is the set $S^2 = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x \cdot x = 1\}$. If a curve $\gamma(t)$ stays on it, then $\gamma \cdot \gamma = 1$ for all $t$; differentiating gives $2\,\gamma(t) \cdot \gamma'(t) = 0$. So every velocity at $x$ is perpendicular to $x$, and the tangent space is the plane $$T_x S^2 = \{v \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x \cdot v = 0\}.$$ The tangent projection $P_x = I - x x^\top$ removes the component of $v$ along $x$: $P_x v = v - (x \cdot v)\,x$. Work out on paper:

  • at the north pole $x = (0, 0, 1)$, project $v = (1, 2, 3)$;
  • at $x = (0.6, 0, 0.8)$, project $v = (0, 0, 1)$, and check that the result is perpendicular to $x$.

Then the cell checks the properties of $P_x$ at a random point, Pythagoras' rule $\|v - t\|^2 = (x\cdot v)^2 + \|P_x v - t\|^2$ for tangent vectors $t$ (so $P_x v$ is the closest tangent vector to $v$), and that velocities of random curves on the sphere are tangent.

2. Charts: flat coordinates for part of the sphere¶

A chart gives coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^2$ to part of the manifold; an atlas is a set of charts that covers all of it, like the pages of a world atlas. Two charts for the sphere:

  • Spherical coordinates $(\theta, \varphi)$, with $\theta$ the angle from the north pole (latitude $= 90° - \theta$) and $\varphi$ the longitude: $x = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi, \sin\theta\sin\varphi, \cos\theta)$. The columns of the Jacobian $J = [\partial x/\partial\theta,\ \partial x/\partial\varphi]$ span the tangent plane. At the poles $\partial x/\partial\varphi = 0$: the chart breaks down there.
  • Stereographic projection from the north pole, $\sigma_N(x) = (x_1, x_2)/(1 - x_3)$, covers everything except the north pole; the projection from the south pole, $\sigma_S(x) = (x_1, x_2)/(1 + x_3)$, covers everything except the south pole. Together they form an atlas. Where both work, the change of chart is $u \mapsto u/\|u\|^2$.

By hand: where do $\sigma_N$ and $\sigma_S$ send the point $(0, 0.6, 0.8)$? Check that $u/\|u\|^2$ turns one answer into the other. No single chart can cover the whole sphere without breaking somewhere: that is why we need atlases, and why every flat world map distorts something.

def sph_to_xyz(theta, phi):                    # theta: angle from the north pole; phi: longitude
    return np.stack([np.sin(theta) * np.cos(phi), np.sin(theta) * np.sin(phi), np.cos(theta)], axis=-1)

def xyz_to_sph(x):
    return np.arccos(np.clip(x[..., 2], -1, 1)), np.arctan2(x[..., 1], x[..., 0]) % (2 * np.pi)

def jac_sph(theta, phi):                       # columns: dx/dtheta and dx/dphi
    return np.array([[np.cos(theta) * np.cos(phi), -np.sin(theta) * np.sin(phi)],
                     [np.cos(theta) * np.sin(phi),  np.sin(theta) * np.cos(phi)],
                     [-np.sin(theta),               0.0]])

pts = rng.normal(size=(1000, 3)); pts /= np.linalg.norm(pts, axis=1, keepdims=True)

print(f"spherical chart: round trip x -> (theta, phi) -> x on 1000 points, largest error {np.abs(sph_to_xyz(th, ph) - pts).max():.1e}")
print(f"the Jacobian columns are tangent: largest |x . dx/dtheta|, |x . dx/dphi| = "
      f"{max(np.abs(sph_to_xyz(t, p) @ jac_sph(t, p)).max() for t, p in zip(th[:200], ph[:200])):.1e}")

def stereo_N(x): return x[..., :2] / (1 - x[..., 2:3])       # misses the north pole
def stereo_S(x): return x[..., :2] / (1 + x[..., 2:3])       # misses the south pole

    return np.concatenate([2 * u, s - 1], axis=-1) / (s + 1)

3. The metric in coordinates¶

A Riemannian metric is an inner product on every tangent space. On the sphere we use the dot product of $\mathbb{R}^3$, restricted to tangent vectors. A velocity $\xi = (\dot\theta, \dot\varphi)$ in a chart becomes the tangent vector $J\xi$, whose squared length is $$\|J\xi\|^2 = \xi^\top G\,\xi, \qquad G = J^\top J \ \ (\text{the metric tensor}).$$ For spherical coordinates, work out $G$ by hand from the two Jacobian columns: you should get $G = \operatorname{diag}(1, \sin^2\theta)$, the familiar $ds^2 = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\, d\varphi^2$. For the stereographic chart, $G = \big(\tfrac{2}{1 + \|u\|^2}\big)^2 I$. For the Mercator chart (longitude $\lambda$ and $y = \ln\tan(\frac\pi4 + \frac{\text{lat}}{2})$), $G = \operatorname{sech}^2(y)\, I = \cos^2(\text{lat})\, I$. When $G$ is a multiple of $I$ the chart is conformal: it keeps angles and stretches lengths equally in all directions at each point. Remember the stereographic factor $2/(1 + \|u\|^2)$: the Poincaré disk of hyperbolic geometry (sessions 4 and 6) has $2/(1 - \|u\|^2)$.

We check all three formulas against $J^\top J$, with $J$ computed by central differences.

def num_jacobian(f, u, h=1e-6):                # central differences, one column per coordinate
    return np.stack([(f(u + h * e) - f(u - h * e)) / (2 * h) for e in np.eye(len(u))], axis=1)

gd = lambda y: 2 * np.arctan(np.exp(y)) - np.pi / 2           # Mercator y -> latitude (the Gudermannian function)
mercator_inv = lambda w: sph_to_xyz(np.pi / 2 - gd(w[1]), w[0])   # (longitude, y) -> point on the sphere

err_sph, err_ste, err_mer = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
for _ in range(300):

    u = rng.normal(size=2) * 1.5
    J = num_jacobian(stereo_N_inv, u); err_ste = max(err_ste, np.abs(J.T @ J - (2 / (1 + u @ u)) ** 2 * np.eye(2)).max())

    J = num_jacobian(mercator_inv, w); err_mer = max(err_mer, np.abs(J.T @ J - np.eye(2) / np.cosh(w[1]) ** 2).max())

4. Curve lengths: in the embedding, with the metric, and wrongly¶

The length of a curve is $\int_0^1 \|\gamma'(t)\|\,dt$. We take a wobbly loop around the north pole given in spherical coordinates, $\theta(t) = 1 + 0.3\sin 4\pi t$, $\varphi(t) = 2\pi t$, and measure its length four ways:

  1. in $\mathbb{R}^3$: the length of a polygon through 200,001 points of the curve;
  2. with the metric of spherical coordinates: $\int \sqrt{\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2}\,dt$;
  3. with the Mercator metric: $\int \operatorname{sech}(y)\sqrt{\dot\lambda^2 + \dot y^2}\,dt$;
  4. wrongly, as if $(\theta, \varphi)$ were a flat plane: $\int \sqrt{\dot\theta^2 + \dot\varphi^2}\,dt$; and as measured on a Mercator map, $\int\sqrt{\dot\lambda^2 + \dot y^2}\,dt$.

By hand first: a latitude circle at angle $\theta_0$ from the pole has $\dot\theta = 0$ and $\dot\varphi = 2\pi$, so its length is $2\pi\sin\theta_0$. How long is the latitude circle through Dhaka (23.76° N) on the Earth, $R = 6{,}371$ km?

theta_c = lambda t: 1.0 + 0.3 * np.sin(4 * np.pi * t)
phi_c = lambda t: 2 * np.pi * t
dtheta = lambda t: 1.2 * np.pi * np.cos(4 * np.pi * t)

5. Mercator: lengths and areas on a map¶

On a Mercator map scaled to be exact on the equator, every latitude circle is drawn with the length of the equator, $2\pi R$. So at latitude "lat", lengths are stretched by the scale factor $k = 1/\cos(\text{lat}) = \sec(\text{lat})$ in every direction (the chart is conformal), and areas by $k^2$. We check $dy/d(\text{lat}) = \sec(\text{lat})$ numerically at Dhaka and tabulate a few latitudes.

6. Pictures: a tangent plane and a vector field¶

Left: the tangent plane at Dhaka, three vectors $v$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$ (grey) and their projections $P_x v$ (red), which lie in the plane. Right: a vector field chooses a tangent vector at every point. Here it is the gradient of the height $f(x) = x_3$ along the sphere, $P_x e_3 = e_3 - x_3 x$, which points uphill towards the north pole and has length $\sin\theta$. It vanishes at both poles: every continuous tangent vector field on the sphere vanishes somewhere (the hairy ball theorem; Course 9 session 3). Projecting a Euclidean gradient onto the tangent plane is exactly how session 5 computes Riemannian gradients.

import numpy as np
import torch
import geoopt
from geomstats.geometry.hypersphere import Hypersphere

sphere = Hypersphere(dim=2)                                                   # the unit sphere in R^3
x = sphere.spherical_to_extrinsic(np.array([np.radians(90 - 23.76), np.radians(90.39)]))   # (theta from north pole, longitude): Dhaka
v = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
pv = sphere.to_tangent(v, x)                                                  # the projection (I - x x^T) v
print(sphere.belongs(x), sphere.is_tangent(pv, x), np.allclose(pv, v - (x @ v) * x))
print(torch.allclose(geoopt.Sphere().proju(torch.tensor(x), torch.tensor(v)), torch.tensor(pv)))   # geoopt's projection agrees

What to notice¶

(Written from the run above; your numbers will match if you keep the seeds.)

  1. Tangent vectors are perpendicular to the point. At the north pole, $P(1, 2, 3) = (1, 2, 0)$; at $x = (0.6, 0, 0.8)$, $P(0, 0, 1) = (-0.48, 0, 0.36)$, perpendicular to $x$. $P = I - xx^\top$ was symmetric and idempotent with eigenvalues 0, 1, 1 (one direction removed, a 2-dimensional tangent plane left), Pythagoras' rule held to 3.6e-15, and the velocities of 100 random curves on the sphere were perpendicular to the point up to 2.9e-10, the finite-difference error.
  2. A chart is only local. The spherical chart's Jacobian had singular values $(1, \sin\theta)$: rank 2 everywhere except at the pole, where it dropped to rank 1 and the chart failed. The two stereographic charts cover the sphere between them; $(0, 0.6, 0.8)$ went to $(0, 3)$ and $(0, 0.3333)$, and the change of chart $u/\|u\|^2$ held to 1.8e-14 on 1,000 points.
  3. The metric tensor is $J^\top J$. It was $\operatorname{diag}(1, \sin^2\theta)$ for spherical coordinates (to 3.3e-16), $(2/(1+\|u\|^2))^2 I$ for the stereographic chart and $\operatorname{sech}^2(y)\, I$ for Mercator (both to about 4e-10, the error of central differences). The last two are multiples of $I$: those charts keep angles.
  4. Lengths agree when the metric is used, and only then. The wobbly loop measured 5.79881822 as a polygon in $\mathbb{R}^3$, with the spherical metric and with the Mercator metric. Pretending $(\theta, \varphi)$ is flat gave 6.8153 (+17.5%), and measuring it on a Mercator map gave 7.0557 (+21.7%).
  5. Mercator stretches by $\sec(\text{lat})$. The latitude circle through Dhaka is 36,637 km long, but the map draws it as long as the equator, 40,030 km: lengths are enlarged by 1.0926 and areas by 1.1938 at Dhaka, by 1.6067 and 2.5816 at London, and by 5.7588 and 33.1634 at 80° N. The numerical derivative $dy/d(\text{lat})$ matched $\sec(\text{lat})$ to 8 decimals.
  6. The height gradient vanishes at both poles. $P_x e_3$ had length 0.0 at both poles and 0.9152 (= cos 23.76°) at Dhaka. Every result agreed with the hand calculations; nothing went against expectation. Session 3 uses these tangent planes to build the exponential and logarithm maps.
