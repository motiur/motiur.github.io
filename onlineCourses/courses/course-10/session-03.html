1. By hand on the circle: two different means¶

On a curved space we can define the mean without addition: the Fréchet mean (or Karcher mean) of points $p_1, \dots, p_N$ is the point $x$ that minimizes $$F(x) = \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^N d(x, p_i)^2,$$ where $d$ is the distance along the space. In $\mathbb{R}^n$ this gives the ordinary average. Work this out on paper for the angles 0°, 0° and 90° on the circle, where $d$ is the arc length: for $x$ between 0° and 90°, $F$ is a quadratic in $x$; where is its minimum? Then compare with session 1's circular mean (the average of the unit vectors, normalized), which is $\operatorname{atan2}(1, 2)$. The two answers differ: the circular mean is an extrinsic mean (computed in the surrounding plane), the Fréchet mean an intrinsic one (computed with distances along the circle).

import time
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.spatial import geometric_slerp

t_start = time.time()
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

arc = np.angle(np.exp(1j * (grid[:, None] - pts[None, :])))       # signed arc length, wrapped to (-pi, pi]
F_circle = 0.5 * np.mean(arc ** 2, axis=1)
x_intr = np.degrees(grid[np.argmin(F_circle)])
x_extr = np.degrees(np.angle(np.exp(1j * pts).mean()))
print(f"0, 0 and 90 degrees: Frechet (intrinsic) mean {x_intr:.2f} degrees; circular (extrinsic) mean {x_extr:.2f} degrees")

plt.legend(fontsize=8); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

2. Exponential and logarithm maps on the sphere¶

On the unit sphere, the geodesic (locally shortest path) leaving $x$ with velocity $v \in T_xS^2$ is the great circle $\gamma(t) = \cos(t\|v\|)\,x + \sin(t\|v\|)\,v/\|v\|$. The exponential map walks along it for unit time: $$\exp_x(v) = \cos(\|v\|)\,x + \sin(\|v\|)\,\frac{v}{\|v\|}.$$ The logarithm map is its inverse: $\log_x(y)$ is the tangent vector at $x$ that points towards $y$, with length the distance $d(x, y) = \arccos(x \cdot y)$: $$\log_x(y) = d(x, y)\,\frac{y - (x\cdot y)\,x}{\|y - (x\cdot y)\,x\|}.$$ It is undefined at the antipode $y = -x$, where every direction gives a shortest path; that set is the cut locus of $x$. For stability we compute the angle as $\operatorname{atan2}(\|y - (x\cdot y)x\|,\ x\cdot y)$ rather than $\arccos(x\cdot y)$ (session 1 showed why). By hand: at the north pole, what are $\exp(\frac\pi2, 0, 0)$, $\exp(\frac{3\pi}{2}, 0, 0)$ and the log of the second answer?

def sphere_exp(x, v):
    n = np.linalg.norm(v)
    return x.copy() if n < 1e-15 else np.cos(n) * x + np.sin(n) * v / n

def sphere_log(x, y):
    u = y - (x @ y) * x
    nu = np.linalg.norm(u)
    return np.zeros_like(x) if nu < 1e-15 else np.arctan2(nu, x @ y) * u / nu

def sphere_dist(x, y):
    return np.arctan2(np.linalg.norm(y - (x @ y) * x), x @ y)

3. The Fréchet mean by Riemannian gradient descent¶

Where $\log_x(p)$ is defined, the gradient of $\frac12 d(x, p)^2$ on the sphere is $-\log_x(p)$, so $$\operatorname{grad} F(x) = -\frac1N\sum_i \log_x(p_i), \qquad x \leftarrow \exp_x\big(-\eta\,\operatorname{grad} F(x)\big) = \exp_x\Big(\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_i \log_x(p_i)\Big).$$ With $\eta = 1$ this is the classical Karcher iteration: average the data in the tangent plane at $x$, then step there along the geodesic (Pennec 2006). We first check the gradient formula with a finite difference along a geodesic, then run the iteration on two synthetic sets of 50 directions: "clustered" (spread about 0.15 rad around the north pole) and "spread out" (about 0.8 rad). Each set is drawn by mapping Gaussian tangent vectors at the north pole through $\exp$ (a wrapped normal). Real data could be, for example, directions of wind or of animal movement. We compare with the normalized Euclidean (extrinsic) mean $\sum_i p_i/\|\sum_i p_i\|$.

def wrapped_normal(mu, sigma, n):
    v = rng.normal(size=(n, 3)) * sigma
    v -= np.outer(v @ mu, mu)                             # Gaussian vectors in the tangent plane at mu
    return np.array([sphere_exp(mu, vi) for vi in v])

def frechet_F(x, P):
    return 0.5 * np.mean([sphere_dist(x, p) ** 2 for p in P])

def karcher_mean(P, x0, eta=1.0, max_iter=500, tol=1e-12):
    x, grad_norms = x0.copy(), []
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        step = np.mean([sphere_log(x, p) for p in P], axis=0)   # = -grad F(x)
        grad_norms.append(np.linalg.norm(step))
        if grad_norms[-1] < tol: break
        x = sphere_exp(x, eta * step)
    return x, np.array(grad_norms)

# check grad (1/2) d(x, p)^2 = -log_x(p) with a finite difference along a geodesic

v0 = rng.normal(size=3); v0 -= (x0 @ v0) * x0
h = 1e-6
fd = (0.5 * sphere_dist(sphere_exp(x0, h * v0), p0) ** 2 - 0.5 * sphere_dist(sphere_exp(x0, -h * v0), p0) ** 2) / (2 * h)
print(f"directional derivative: finite difference {fd:.8f}, formula <-log_x(p), v> = {-sphere_log(x0, p0) @ v0:.8f}")

data, runs = {}, {}

    m2, g2 = karcher_mean(P, P[0])                                # a different start

    print(f"{name:10s}: farthest point {np.degrees(max(sphere_dist(m1, p) for p in P)):.1f} degrees from the mean; Karcher iterations "
          f"{len(g1) - 1} (from the extrinsic mean) and {len(g2) - 1} (from a data point), the two results {np.degrees(sphere_dist(m1, m2)):.1e} degrees apart")
    print(f"{'':10s}  Karcher mean vs extrinsic mean: {np.degrees(sphere_dist(m1, extrinsic)):.4f} degrees apart; "
          f"F = {frechet_F(m1, P):.6f} (Karcher) and {frechet_F(extrinsic, P):.6f} (extrinsic)")

4. When the mean is not unique¶

The Fréchet mean always exists on the sphere (a continuous function on a compact space has a minimum), but it need not be unique, and gradient descent finds a local minimum near its start. A sufficient condition for uniqueness: all points lie inside a geodesic ball of radius less than $\pi/2$ (Afsari 2011; Pennec 2006 reviews these conditions). For finitely many points this is the same as lying inside some open hemisphere, which we can test with a small linear program: find a unit direction $c$ with $c \cdot p_i > 0$ for every point. Then a case where uniqueness truly fails: six points evenly spaced on the equator. By symmetry both poles are candidates; by hand, what is $F$ at each pole, and at a data point on the equator?

from scipy.optimize import linprog

def hemisphere_margin(P):        # max over |c_k| <= 1 of min_i c . p_i; positive means all points lie in an open hemisphere
    res = linprog(c=[0, 0, 0, -1], A_ub=np.hstack([-P, np.ones((len(P), 1))]), b_ub=np.zeros(len(P)),
                  bounds=[(-1, 1)] * 3 + [(None, 1)])
    return -res.fun

def describe_margin(m):
    return f"{m:.4f}: inside an open hemisphere" if m > 1e-9 else "0: no open hemisphere contains all the points"

for name in data:
    print(f"{name:10s}: open-hemisphere margin {describe_margin(hemisphere_margin(data[name][0]))}")

5. Parallel transport along a geodesic¶

To compare tangent vectors at different points we must move them between tangent planes. Parallel transport moves a vector along a curve while keeping it "as constant as possible": on a surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$, its derivative along the curve has no tangential part. Along the geodesic from $x$ to $y$ on the sphere it has a closed form (for $y \neq -x$): $$\Gamma_{x\to y}(v) = v - \frac{y\cdot v}{1 + x\cdot y}\,(x + y).$$ It keeps lengths and angles, and it carries the geodesic's own velocity $\log_x(y)$ to its velocity at $y$, which is $-\log_y(x)$. A simple numerical version follows any curve in small steps: project the vector onto the next tangent plane and restore its length.

def transport(x, y, v):                        # parallel transport along the geodesic from x to y
    return v - (y @ v) / (1 + x @ y) * (x + y)

def transport_by_projection(path, v):          # small steps: project onto the next tangent plane, keep the length
    for p in path[1:]:
        n = np.linalg.norm(v); v = v - (p @ v) * p; v *= n / np.linalg.norm(v)
    return v

def geodesic_path(a, b, m):
    return np.array([sphere_exp(a, t * sphere_log(a, b)) for t in np.linspace(0, 1, m + 1)])

err = dict(norm=0.0, tangent=0.0, inner=0.0, velocity=0.0)
for _ in range(500):
    x, y = rng.normal(size=(2, 3)); x /= np.linalg.norm(x); y /= np.linalg.norm(y)

6. Holonomy: around a triangle and back¶

Carry a vector around a closed loop by parallel transport and it comes back turned. On the unit sphere (curvature 1) the turning angle equals the enclosed area, and the angles of a geodesic triangle add up to $\pi$ + area (Gauss–Bonnet; do Carmo 1976). By hand: take the octant triangle with corners $e_3$ (north pole), $e_1$ and $e_2$ on the equator. Its three angles are right angles, so its area is $3\pi/2 - \pi = \pi/2$, one eighth of the sphere ($4\pi/8$). Start at $e_3$ with $v = e_1$ and follow it: along the first edge it is the forward velocity, so it arrives at $e_1$ as $-e_3$; along the equator it is perpendicular to the edge and stays $-e_3$; along the last edge it is backward-pointing and arrives at $e_3$ as $e_2$. It has turned by 90°.

The cell checks this with the projection method for a vector at 45° to the first edge (then both parts of the vector change along every edge) and shows the error shrinking as the steps get smaller. Then, for small random triangles, it compares three numbers computed independently: the angle sum minus $\pi$ (angles from log maps), the area (from the formula $\tan\frac{E}{2} = \frac{|a\cdot(b\times c)|}{1 + a\cdot b + b\cdot c + c\cdot a}$), and the holonomy angle (closed-form transport around the triangle). The loops go counterclockwise seen from outside, and the vector turns counterclockwise.

e1, e2, e3 = np.eye(3)
def signed_angle(u, w, normal):               # angle from u to w, counterclockwise around 'normal'
    return np.arctan2(normal @ np.cross(u, w), u @ w)

w = e1.copy()
for a, b in [(e3, e1), (e1, e2), (e2, e3)]:
    w = transport(a, b, w)

def triangle_numbers(a, b, c):
    if a @ np.cross(b, c) < 0: b, c = c, b                 # counterclockwise seen from outside
    angle = lambda A, B, C: np.arctan2(np.linalg.norm(np.cross(sphere_log(A, B), sphere_log(A, C))), sphere_log(A, B) @ sphere_log(A, C))
    excess = angle(a, b, c) + angle(b, c, a) + angle(c, a, b) - np.pi
    area = 2 * np.arctan2(abs(a @ np.cross(b, c)), 1 + a @ b + b @ c + c @ a)
    t = sphere_log(a, b) / np.linalg.norm(sphere_log(a, b)); w = t.copy()
    for P_, Q_ in [(a, b), (b, c), (c, a)]: w = transport(P_, Q_, w)
    return excess, area, signed_angle(t, w, a)

7. The same with geomstats¶

geomstats has the sphere's exp, log, parallel transport and a Fréchet mean estimator. It is not installed here, so this cell is NOT RUN (in Colab or Kaggle, run pip install geomstats first).

# NOT RUN: needs geomstats (pip install geomstats), which is not installed here
import numpy as np
from geomstats.geometry.hypersphere import Hypersphere
from geomstats.learning.frechet_mean import FrechetMean

sphere = Hypersphere(dim=2)
north = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 1.0])
v = np.array([np.pi / 2, 0.0, 0.0])
print(sphere.metric.exp(v, north))                          # (1, 0, 0)
print(sphere.metric.log(np.array([1.0, 0, 0]), north))      # (pi/2, 0, 0)
print(sphere.metric.parallel_transport(np.array([1.0, 0, 0]), north, end_point=np.array([1.0, 0, 0])))   # (0, 0, -1)

X = sphere.random_von_mises_fisher(mu=north, kappa=40, n_samples=50)
mean = FrechetMean(sphere).fit(X)
print(mean.estimate_, X.sum(axis=0) / np.linalg.norm(X.sum(axis=0)))   # Frechet mean and extrinsic mean: close for clustered data

What to notice¶

(Written from the run above; your numbers will match if you keep the seeds.)

  1. Intrinsic and extrinsic means differ. For 0°, 0° and 90° on the circle, the Fréchet mean is 30.00° (the minimum of a quadratic, as on paper) and the circular mean 26.57°. Both are reasonable; they answer different questions.
  2. Exp and log are inverse, up to the cut locus. $\exp_N(\frac\pi2, 0, 0) = (1, 0, 0)$ and $\exp_N(\frac{3\pi}{2}, 0, 0) = (-1, 0, 0)$, but the log of the latter has length 1.5708, not $3\pi/2$: walking past the cut locus loses the information. Round trips, tangency and $d = \|\log\|$ held to about 2e-14 on 1,000 random cases, and our geodesics matched scipy.spatial.geometric_slerp to 1.7e-16. Next to the antipode, two points 2e-9 apart had logs of length 3.141593 pointing in opposite directions: log is discontinuous there.
  3. Riemannian gradient descent finds the Fréchet mean. The finite difference of $\frac12 d^2$ along a geodesic matched $\langle -\log_x p, v\rangle$ (−0.54644084 both ways). The clustered set (farthest point 29.4° from the mean) converged in 5 iterations, and its Karcher and extrinsic means were only 0.0241° apart. The spread-out set (farthest point 129.7° away) needed 21 iterations, and the two means were 1.4952° apart; the Karcher mean has the smaller $F$ (0.685297 against 0.685538), as it must.
  4. Uniqueness is not guaranteed, and the runs show both sides. The spread-out set lies in no open hemisphere, so the uniqueness theorem does not apply, yet two different starts reached the same point (1.2e-10 degrees apart): no theorem promised that. For six points on the equator, the start decided the answer: the north pole from a northern start, the south pole from a southern one, both with $F$ = 1.233701 = $\pi^2/8$ (a data point on the equator has $F$ = 1.736319). The two convergence curves lie exactly on top of each other: each took 41 iterations, shrinking the gradient by about half per step.
  5. Parallel transport keeps lengths and angles. On 500 random pairs, lengths, inner products and tangency were kept to about 4e-14, and the geodesic's velocity was carried to the velocity at the end. By hand, $(0, 1, 0)$ at $(1, 0, 0)$ moves to $(-0.6, 0.64, -0.48)$ at $(0, 0.6, 0.8)$; 2,000 projection steps gave $(-0.599763, 0.640142, -0.480107)$.
  6. Holonomy equals the enclosed area. Around the octant triangle, $e_1$ came back as $e_2$, turned by 90.0000°. The projection method turned a 45° vector by 93.5399°, 90.3534°, 90.0353° and 90.0035° with 10 to 10,000 steps per edge: the error shrinks in proportion to the step size. For six small random triangles, the angle sum minus 180°, the area (times $180/\pi$) and the holonomy agreed to 3.1e-14 degrees (Gauss–Bonnet with curvature 1).
