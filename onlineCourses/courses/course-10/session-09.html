1. SPD matrices and four distances¶

A symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrix has only positive eigenvalues; covariance matrices are SPD (or nearly so). The SPD matrices form an open cone: $A + B$ and $tA$ ($t > 0$) stay SPD, but $A - B$ need not. Functions of an SPD matrix act on its eigenvalues: if $A = V\,\text{diag}(\lambda)\,V^\top$, then $\log A = V\,\text{diag}(\log\lambda)\,V^\top$, and likewise for $\exp$, $A^{1/2}$ and $A^{-1/2}$. We compute them with np.linalg.eigh and check them against SciPy.

Four distances between SPD matrices $A$ and $B$:

  • Euclidean (Frobenius): $\|A - B\|_F$;
  • log-Euclidean (Arsigny et al. 2007): $\|\log A - \log B\|_F$;
  • affine-invariant (Pennec et al. 2006): $\|\log(A^{-1/2} B A^{-1/2})\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_i \log^2 \lambda_i}$, where $\lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues of $A^{-1}B$ (the generalized eigenvalues of the pair $B, A$);
  • Bures–Wasserstein (Bhatia et al. 2019): $\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}A + \operatorname{tr}B - 2\operatorname{tr}(A^{1/2} B A^{1/2})^{1/2}}$, the optimal-transport distance between the Gaussians $N(0, A)$ and $N(0, B)$.

The cell checks the two formulas for the affine-invariant distance against each other, and which distances stay the same under a congruence $A \mapsto WAW^\top$, the change that a different mixing of sources into sensors (or a change of units) causes. Last, it moves towards the boundary of the cone: from $I$ to $\text{diag}(1, \varepsilon)$, which becomes singular as $\varepsilon \to 0$.

import time
import numpy as np
import scipy.linalg as sla
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold

t_start = time.time()
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def fun(A, f):                                                # apply f to the eigenvalues; works on stacks of matrices
    w, V = np.linalg.eigh(A)
    return (V * f(w)[..., None, :]) @ np.swapaxes(V, -1, -2)
logm = lambda A: fun(A, np.log)
expm = lambda A: fun(A, np.exp)
sqrtm = lambda A: fun(A, np.sqrt)
isqrtm = lambda A: fun(A, lambda w: 1 / np.sqrt(w))

def d_euc(A, B): return np.linalg.norm(A - B)
def d_le(A, B):  return np.linalg.norm(logm(A) - logm(B))
def d_ai(A, B):  return np.sqrt(np.sum(np.log(sla.eigh(B, A, eigvals_only=True)) ** 2))
def d_bw(A, B):
    s = sqrtm(A)
    return np.sqrt(max(np.trace(A) + np.trace(B) - 2 * np.trace(sqrtm(s @ B @ s)), 0.0))

def random_spd(n, rng):
    X = rng.normal(size=(n, 2 * n))
    return X @ X.T / (2 * n) + 0.1 * np.eye(n)

2. Means, and the swelling effect¶

The affine-invariant (Karcher) mean of $C_1, \dots, C_N$ minimizes $\sum_i d_{AI}(M, C_i)^2$. At the minimum the tangent vectors to the data average to zero, $\sum_i \log(M^{-1/2} C_i M^{-1/2}) = 0$, which suggests the fixed-point iteration (session 3's Riemannian gradient descent with step 1)

$$M \leftarrow M^{1/2} \exp\Big(\frac1N \sum_i \log(M^{-1/2} C_i M^{-1/2})\Big) M^{1/2}.$$

The log-Euclidean mean is $\exp\big(\frac1N\sum_i \log C_i\big)$, with no iteration. Arsigny et al. (2007) note that the two means have the same determinant but differ in general; the cell checks both claims, and that the affine-invariant mean moves with a congruence: the mean of $WC_iW^\top$ is $W M W^\top$.

Swelling. Take $A = \text{diag}(4, 0.25)$ and $B$, the same ellipse turned by 60°; both have determinant 1. Along the straight line $(1-t)A + tB$ the determinant grows, so the Euclidean average of two "thin" covariances is a "fat" one. Along the affine-invariant geodesic $A^{1/2}(A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2})^t A^{1/2}$ and the log-Euclidean path $\exp((1-t)\log A + t\log B)$, the determinant is $\det(A)^{1-t}\det(B)^t = 1$ throughout.

def mean_ai(Cs, iters=100, tol=1e-12, history=None):
    M = Cs.mean(0)                                            # start at the Euclidean mean
    for k in range(iters):
        s, si = sqrtm(M), isqrtm(M)
        L = logm(si @ Cs @ si).mean(0)
        if history is not None:
            history.append(np.linalg.norm(L))
        M = s @ expm(L) @ s
        if np.linalg.norm(L) < tol:
            break
    return M

def mean_le(Cs):
    return expm(logm(Cs).mean(0))

Cs = np.array([random_spd(8, rng) for _ in range(20)])
hist = []

3. Synthetic EEG-like trials¶

Motor-imagery brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) classify short EEG trials by the power of brain rhythms: imagining a left-hand movement lowers the mu-rhythm power over one hemisphere (event-related desynchronization), and imagining the right hand lowers it over the other. A trial is summarized by its channel covariance matrix after band-pass filtering.

Our synthetic version: 8 sources with random powers (log-normal, varying from trial to trial) are mixed into 8 channels by a mixing matrix, plus sensor noise, for 128 time samples per trial. In class 0 the power of source 0 drops to half; in class 1, source 1's does. Each of 8 subjects has its own mixing matrix (a shared "head model" plus a personal change and channel gains) and its own overall power, and 50 trials per class. Real data would be a public motor-imagery dataset such as BCI Competition IV 2a (section 7, NOT RUN).

n_ch, T, n_sub, n_per = 8, 128, 8, 50
A0 = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=(n_ch, n_ch))      # shared head model

def make_subject(seed):
    r = np.random.default_rng(100 + seed)
    gains = np.diag(np.exp(r.normal(0, 0.3, n_ch)))          # channel gains (electrode contact)
    A = gains @ (A0 + 0.4 * r.normal(size=(n_ch, n_ch)))      # personal mixing
    level = np.exp(r.normal(0, 0.5))                          # overall power
    covs, labels = [], []
    for y in [0, 1]:
        for _ in range(n_per):
            p = np.exp(r.normal(0, 0.4, n_ch)) * level
            p[y] *= 0.5                                       # desynchronization of source 0 or 1
            X = (r.normal(size=(T, n_ch)) * np.sqrt(p)) @ A.T + r.normal(0, 0.3 * np.sqrt(level), (T, n_ch))
            covs.append(X.T @ X / T); labels.append(y)
    return np.array(covs), np.array(labels)

subjects = [make_subject(s) for s in range(n_sub)]
C_all = np.concatenate([c for c, _ in subjects])
print(f"{n_sub} subjects x {2 * n_per} trials, covariance matrices of size {C_all.shape[1:]}")
print(f"smallest eigenvalue over all trials {np.linalg.eigvalsh(C_all).min():.3f}; condition numbers from "
      f"{np.linalg.cond(C_all).min():.0f} to {np.linalg.cond(C_all).max():.0f}")
print("log-determinant of each subject's mean covariance:", " ".join(f"{np.linalg.slogdet(mean_ai(c))[1]:.2f}" for c, _ in subjects))

4. Classifiers: minimum distance to mean, and the tangent space¶

Minimum distance to mean (MDM) (Barachant et al. 2012): compute the mean covariance of each class, and assign a trial to the class whose mean is nearest. With the Euclidean distance and mean this is a plain nearest-centroid rule; with the affine-invariant ones it is Barachant et al.'s Riemannian classifier.

Tangent space + logistic regression: map every matrix to the tangent space at the overall affine-invariant mean $M$, $S = \log(M^{-1/2} C M^{-1/2})$, and keep its 36 upper-triangular entries, with the off-diagonal ones multiplied by $\sqrt2$ so that Euclidean lengths of the vectors equal Frobenius norms of $S$. Then any Euclidean classifier works (Barachant et al. 2012 used LDA; we use logistic regression).

Within each subject we use 5-fold cross-validation and average over the 8 subjects.

iu = np.triu_indices(n_ch)
wts = np.where(iu[0] == iu[1], 1.0, np.sqrt(2))

def tangent_vectors(Cs, M):
    si = isqrtm(M)
    return logm(si @ Cs @ si)[:, iu[0], iu[1]] * wts

def predict_mdm(Ctr, ytr, Cte, metric):
    mean, d = {"Euclidean": (lambda c: c.mean(0), d_euc), "log-Euclidean": (mean_le, d_le),
               "affine-invariant": (mean_ai, d_ai)}[metric]
    centres = [mean(Ctr[ytr == k]) for k in [0, 1]]
    return np.array([np.argmin([d(M, C) for M in centres]) for C in Cte])

def predict_ts(Ctr, ytr, Cte):
    M = mean_ai(Ctr)
    clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000).fit(tangent_vectors(Ctr, M), ytr)
    return clf.predict(tangent_vectors(Cte, M))

methods = ["MDM Euclidean", "MDM log-Euclidean", "MDM affine-invariant", "tangent space + LR"]
def accuracies(Ctr, ytr, Cte, yte):
    out = [np.mean(predict_mdm(Ctr, ytr, Cte, m.split(" ", 1)[1]) == yte) for m in methods[:3]]
    return out + [np.mean(predict_ts(Ctr, ytr, Cte) == yte)]

t0 = time.time()
within = []
for C, y in subjects:
    folds = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0).split(C, y)
    within.append(np.mean([accuracies(C[tr], y[tr], C[te], y[te]) for tr, te in folds], axis=0))
within = np.array(within)
print("within-subject accuracy (5-fold CV), mean over 8 subjects [lowest subject, highest subject]:")
for k, m in enumerate(methods):
    print(f"  {m:22s} {within[:, k].mean():.3f}  [{within[:, k].min():.3f}, {within[:, k].max():.3f}]")
print(f"({time.time() - t0:.1f} s)")

5. Across subjects: recentring¶

Now train on 7 subjects and test on the 8th (leave one subject out). Each subject's covariances differ by a congruence-like change (mixing and gains) and a scale, so the classes of different subjects sit in different places on the manifold.

Recentring (Zanini et al. 2018): for each subject, compute the affine-invariant mean $M_s$ of all its trials, ignoring the labels, and replace each trial by $M_s^{-1/2} C M_s^{-1/2}$. Every subject's mean becomes the identity. Because it uses no labels, it can also be applied to the test subject's trials. If two subjects differed by an exact congruence $C \mapsto WCW^\top$, recentring would leave only an orthogonal rotation between them (you can prove this from the affine invariance of the mean).

def recentre(C):
    si = isqrtm(mean_ai(C))
    return si @ C @ si

t0 = time.time()
cross = {}
for rc in [False, True]:
    subs = [(recentre(C) if rc else C, y) for C, y in subjects]
    accs = []
    for i in range(n_sub):
        Ctr = np.concatenate([subs[j][0] for j in range(n_sub) if j != i])
        ytr = np.concatenate([subs[j][1] for j in range(n_sub) if j != i])
        accs.append(accuracies(Ctr, ytr, *subs[i]))
    cross[rc] = np.array(accs)
print("leave-one-subject-out accuracy, mean over 8 test subjects [lowest, highest]:")
for k, m in enumerate(methods):

6. SPDNet's layers in a few lines¶

SPDNet (Huang & Van Gool 2017) is a network whose hidden values are SPD matrices:

  • BiMap: $X \mapsto W^\top X W$ with $W \in \mathbb{R}^{8 \times 4}$ having orthonormal columns (a point on the Stiefel manifold, session 5), which keeps the output SPD and shrinks its size;
  • ReEig: $U \max(\varepsilon, \Sigma) U^\top$, which raises eigenvalues below $\varepsilon$, a ReLU-like nonlinearity that also keeps the matrix away from singular;
  • LogEig: $U \log(\Sigma) U^\top$, after which ordinary layers can follow.

The cell runs one trial through the three layers and checks what each promises. Training $W$ needs Riemannian steps on the Stiefel manifold (TODO 2).

C = subjects[0][0][0]
Wst = np.linalg.qr(np.random.default_rng(1).normal(size=(n_ch, 4)))[0]     # 8 x 4, orthonormal columns
X1 = Wst.T @ C @ Wst
eps = np.quantile(np.linalg.eigvalsh(X1), 0.5)                            # a large floor, to make ReEig visible

What to notice¶

(Written from the run above; your numbers will match if you keep the seeds, except the timings.)

  1. The formulas agree. The eigenvalue-based matrix functions matched SciPy to 1.8e-14 or better, and both formulas for the affine-invariant distance gave 3.336229.
  2. Only the affine-invariant distance ignores the mixing. Under a random congruence $WAW^\top$ it changed by 2.75e-13, and under inversion by 4.9e-15. The log-Euclidean and Bures–Wasserstein distances did not change under the orthogonal $Q$ but changed by 0.919 and 2.39 under $W$, and the Euclidean distance changed by 36.5 under $W$.
  3. The Euclidean metric puts singular matrices at a finite distance. From $I$ to $\text{diag}(1, \varepsilon)$ the Euclidean distance never exceeded 1.0000, while the log-Euclidean and affine-invariant distances grew like $|\log\varepsilon|$, to 20.723 at $\varepsilon = 10^{-9}$.
  4. Means. The Karcher iteration converged linearly, the average tangent vector shrinking by a factor of about 10 each step (from 7.1e-01 to 2.5e-08 in 8 steps). The affine-invariant and log-Euclidean means had the same determinant, 0.2545562, as Arsigny et al. state, yet were 0.0293 apart. The Euclidean mean's determinant was 1.82, about 7 times larger: swelling. Along the line from $A$ to $B$ the determinant reached 3.6367 at the midpoint, against 1.0000 on both curved paths.
  5. Within a subject, geometry helped. MDM with the log-Euclidean (0.840) or affine-invariant (0.839) metric and the tangent-space classifier (0.840) beat Euclidean MDM (0.730) by about 11 points. The three Riemannian methods tied; the tangent space did not beat MDM here, with only 80 training trials for 36 features.
  6. Across subjects, recentring mattered more than the metric. Without it, accuracy fell to 0.649–0.705, with some test subjects near chance (lowest 0.520). With it, all four methods reached 0.820–0.826. Recentring moves every subject's matrices close to the identity, where the curved and flat distances nearly agree, so even Euclidean MDM reached 0.820, more than its within-subject 0.730 because it now trained on 700 trials from 7 subjects. Against expectation, without recentring the affine-invariant MDM (0.676) was not the best; the log-Euclidean one (0.705) was.
  7. SPDNet's layers do what they promise: BiMap kept the matrix SPD with $W^\top W = I$ to 2.2e-16, ReEig raised only the eigenvalues below the floor, and LogEig returned a symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues were the logarithms.
